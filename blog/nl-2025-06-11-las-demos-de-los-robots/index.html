📹 Robot demos

The latest demo of Figure's robot seems to have no tricks up its sleeve.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 11 June 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

The video published last week by Brett Adcock, CEO of Figure, is going to mark a before and after for all those who hope to soon have humanoid robots in their factories, shops or homes.

For the first time, a video makes it very clear that humanoid robots are only a few years away from reaching the market.

Why do I say this?

Because a few days earlier Figure showed us the same scene but with the robot held by a crane and plugged into the mains with a cable, but now we see it 100% free, as it should be when it is working in the real world.

In this way you understand that the video is very real; they have even let it be seen that the robot makes some mistakes, without hiding it.

We saw something similar when the people at Agility showed us their Digit robot running for 7 hours; they even let us see when the robot falls to the ground and the company's operators have to pick it up.

I mention this because when I have meetings with clients who manufacture all kinds of products, where significant human labour is still essential, their faces change when I show them these videos, because they finally visualize that the solution to their staff shortage problem may be near.

And the solution certainly does not involve spending a fortune on a system of robotic arms that also requires changes to the structure of their facilities. The solution is to prepare for the arrival of humanoids, which is imminent.

The West has had rock-bottom productivity for too many years; humanoid robots are going to be the lever we need to move to the next level.

1X now has its own VLA

But before robots invade the world, the Artificial Intelligence-based software layer will need to be developed even further, something 1X has made very clear by presenting its Redwood model, a VLA that would be equivalent to the one presented months ago by Figure called Helix.

Pay attention to the mission the Norwegian company has just set out:

"*To transform the quality of life of older people and busy parents, and to develop use cases unimaginable today*"

This is a strong sign that this company is focused on assistance in the home, something I am also encountering more and more often. It is no longer only SMEs demanding this technology, but also families with dependent members or an excessive workload, who hope to soon have humanoid robots that lighten their work.

Will the company, whose robots seem inspired by IKEA design, be able to satisfy this market need?

The demo they have presented seems much less realistic than Figure's, although it also involves much more complexity, since it has to perform very varied tasks. In any case, what matters is to look at what they tell us about how they developed this VLA with the following capabilities:

  • Generalization: it is able to handle different types of tasks, such as picking up never-before-seen objects in unfamiliar places. To this end it has been trained on a broad dataset of teleoperated and autonomous situations with the EVE and NEO robots. In addition, Redwood has shown emergent behaviours such as decision-making.
  • Whole-body and multi-contact manipulation: Redwood is one of the first VLAs that controls locomotion jointly with manipulation, enabling leaning and bracing behaviours while manipulating objects.
  • Mobile bimanual manipulation: which allows NEO to position itself precisely to perform tasks, carry out actions that require moving through space and manipulate objects while in motion.
  • Onboard computer: Redwood is computationally efficient and runs entirely on NEO's integrated GPU.

Note: no trace of OpenAI's collaboration in this advance in the development of 1X's VLA. Let's not forget that Sam Altman's company has invested in both Figure and 1X, but apparently it did not take part in the development of either Helix or Redwood. This may mean two things: at OpenAI they no longer care about robotics, or quite the opposite, they are creating their own robot.

🕶️ Imitation learning

I'll pick up on what they say about the model having been trained with data from teleoperated situations, because the next thing I wanted to talk to you about is imitation learning, a technique widely used in AI and based on the psychology of learning, specifically on the observational learning proposed by Albert Bandura.

Through imitation learning, the model observes demonstrations by an expert (human or machine) and learns to reproduce those actions. It can be implemented through direct supervised learning, inverse reinforcement learning or hybrid approaches. It is often trained with sensors, video data or motion recordings.

As you can imagine, imitation learning will be key to teaching skills to humanoid robots without the need for manual coding or exhaustive simulation. It will also allow the natural transfer of knowledge from humans to machines through gestures, physical demonstrations or language, facilitating much more intuitive and efficient collaboration.

This is the reason why in many of the demos we see of robot training, for example those by NVIDIA and Google, we always see people equipped with virtual or augmented reality glasses, performing everyday tasks so that this information can later be processed by AI and used by robots.

📦 Logistics as a testing ground

Another piece of news this week has been Amazon's move to test humanoids in the delivery of its products, which makes perfect sense, especially if we see that companies like Figure and Agility have started working first precisely with logistics companies.

In Amazon's case, the information that has leaked is that its research centre, called Lab126, is working on developing AI agents that can increase robots' intelligence, so that they are not only devoted to performing pre-programmed warehouse tasks but can even venture into home delivery work.

To make this possible, the company would not only be working on developing its own hardware but is also testing some robot models, such as Unitree's. A strategy reminiscent of the one previously applied with drones and other types of autonomous vehicles, but which in this case seems to make much more sense, since the humanoid is much more versatile in adapting to the different situations that may arise in this last-mile delivery task.
