Digital twins of robots

While progress is being made on the physical side of robots, there is also a lot of work on their virtual simulation.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 23 April 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Scaling robot training seems to be one of the great challenges the robotics industry is tackling with all its energy, and the advances are very significant. And although much remains to be done, everything indicates that things are moving in the right direction, which will lead to those very ambitious estimates of millions of robots working around the world in the coming years being fulfilled.

But for this to happen, a great deal of research will need to keep taking place and robot manufacturers will need to keep incorporating it, both in hardware and in software, which is what we are going to talk about here today.

Let's start by presenting the RHyME project, being developed at Cornell University, which proposes a model called One-Shot Imitation under Mismatched Execution, focused on enabling robots to learn certain movements and tasks by watching, just once, a demonstration video in which a person performs the action.

The concept of One-shot imitation under mismatched execution explains that the robot only needs to see one demonstration by the human in order to learn, even if there are physical or movement-style mismatches. Thus RHyME is a system that allows robots to learn to imitate what a person does in a video, even if there are large differences between the human's body and the robot's (for example, a human hand versus a robotic gripper) or in how they move.

This makes us ask: why is it so difficult for robots to imitate humans? And this is where we find the embodiment mismatch, because the human's body and the robot's are not the same; the different movement styles, since a human moves the hand in a way the robot cannot imitate exactly; and the lack of paired data, because it is difficult to have a video of a human doing something and another of the robot doing exactly the same thing in order to synchronize them.

In this case RHyME does not need the human video and the robot video to be paired; instead, what it does is look for parts of human videos that make sense for the task, join those pieces to create a video that represents the task well, and use that composite video to train the robot.

Another initiative worth mentioning regarding how software is being created for robot training is Reborn, which we learned about through the announcement of its collaboration with Booster Robotics to work together on the development of humanoid robots, the former contributing its decentralized platform for generating humanoid robot data and the latter its small robots focused on demonstrating physical skills in sport.

Through this collaboration, Reborn will provide Booster with:

  1. Large-scale human motion data, collected through a hybrid process combining Rebocap™ and the Reborn virtual reality gaming platform. Rebocap captures full-body motion with high fidelity, while virtual reality provides detailed hand manipulation data. This diverse dataset enables scalable imitation learning in real-world tasks such as walking, grasping, assembling and using tools.
  2. Synthetic simulation data backed by Roboverse, which Booster Robotics will be able to use, including thousands of procedurally generated humanoid behaviours such as walking, keeping balance, navigating obstacles and manipulating complex objects. This data has been created in high-fidelity physics environments with labelled joint and task information. Resources that lower the training barriers for robots' fundamental motor skills.
  3. Versatile AI model services developed in-house for humanoids, including whole-body control, vision-language-action (VLA) and dexterous hand grasping.

All this leads us to delve into the concept of digital twins applied to the development of robotics, a technique that is already widely used in industry in general, but which takes on special importance when we talk about humanoid robots because of the enormous complexity they present in meeting the expectations placed on their work.

A digital twin is a virtual replica of a physical system, in this case a humanoid robot, which allows us to simulate its behaviour, environment and internal processes in real time. To do so, it draws on real-world data and AI models to predict, diagnose and optimize the robot's performance before executing actions in the physical world.

In this way, digital twins make it possible to train and test control and perception algorithms without the physical risk involved in the robot actually acting before its programming is well polished, which facilitates the development of safer, more efficient and more adaptive humanoid robots.

Digital twins can also be used to monitor the robot's state, detect failures before they occur and optimize its tasks in real environments. We thus see that digital twins are beginning to be integrated with generative AI models and predictive models, allowing humanoid robots to learn in virtual environments before acting in the real world. This capability will reduce costs, accelerate deployment and increase the reliability of these machines in all kinds of activities and environments.

And to finish, let's look at some examples of how different companies are contributing to the development of humanoid robots through simulators and digital twins:

  • Genie 2 from Deepmind is a world model capable of generating an infinite variety of playable, action-controllable 3D environments for training and evaluating embodied agents, among which we already know robots are included, as Demis Hassabis recently explained in an interview.
  • Cosmos from NVIDIA is a platform of generative world foundation models (WFMs), advanced tokenizers, guardrails and an accelerated data processing and curation pipeline built to accelerate the development of physical AI, in technologies such as autonomous vehicles and robots.
  • Reachy2 from Pollen, now owned by Hugging Face, offers a Docker simulator so that a digital twin of the robot can be used for educational, demonstration, testing or development purposes.
