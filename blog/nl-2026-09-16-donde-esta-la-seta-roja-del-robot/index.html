Where is the humanoid robot's big red button?

The importance of robots having an emergency stop button

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 16 September 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

Version 5 of Agility's Digit humanoid robot has just been presented, and the main message conveyed in their communication is the safety the robot offers when working in the same environment as people.

When a company with Agility's track record decides to put the focus on safety, it is because it sees that it can be a differentiating value compared with what its competitors are doing. And it is normal, because in the case of Unitree, whose robots are the best known, the strategy is very different, which means situations are arising in which some of the robot's actions can be seen to be putting people's safety at risk.

At this point the first thing to clarify is that many of the videos circulating out there of a robot making dangerous movements, even where it seems to attack people, may be staged or simulated, but in any case, this does not mean we shouldn't worry about this safety issue being properly resolved.

It is also necessary to clarify here that, since most of the robots that can now be bought, mainly those from Unitree and Agibot, are mainly controlled from a remote control, that is where we find the safety options for switching off the robot in case something unexpected happens. But in any case, robotics experts hope there can be other, more suitable safety options that do not depend on a single person.

So, going back to Digit, safety is part of the robot's design, not an add-on that comes afterwards once everything else is solved.

Image: Image

This is how the company itself presents it:

"After a decade of research and years of deployment experience, our fifth-generation humanoid robot is the most powerful automation tool we have ever created. Designed for collaborative safety, to work in close proximity with people and adaptable to handle complete workflows".

What happens is that being "designed" for safety means having to make some concessions that, in the long term, it seems will be the way robots really work. I understand it is a transitional situation until this industry is much more developed, but it would make no sense that, when everything is full of robots, they have to stop and crouch down when a person approaches them.

With this introduction to the topic of safety done, what I would like to discuss next are two things that are closely related and are not directly about robotics but about AI.

1️⃣ The media drama about the need to slow down the pace of AI development is going to remain an anecdote when Physical AI is in full swing, because if we are already worried about an Agent going around the Internet doing things without control, you can't imagine the problem it will be when a robot does the same thing 😱. But don't worry, the pace of development is not going to stop, because society does not want to lose the opportunity for AI to help us cure diseases or other kinds of problems still to be solved, or rather, the market is not willing to miss this great opportunity, and companies will continue advancing in the development of this technology that can bring so much benefit. Along the way there will be upsets, but the journey will have been worth it. And if my speech doesn't convince you, you can read this one, which is much better.

2️⃣ Regulation, however much we insist on it, is not going to solve anything 🥸. In fact, at this stage nothing is solved with regulation; the only thing that keeps us afloat as a society is good education. I mean that, although it is attempted at an institutional level, we have crossed a line where technology is so far ahead of regulation that any attempt at control will be completely obsolete. For example, the AI law in Europe focuses on controlling models and how they are used, but leaves out what happens with robots, which for better or worse are going to have a much greater impact, since they are a physical thing and not just digital. And in this case, as with AI, robots are coming to help us solve problems, such as the labour shortage in industry and services, so it is inevitable that they will end up arriving.

My conclusion is that what we should be working on most at the moment is education, but I don't mean formal education; I mean education in values. The education system has long stopped preparing people for the working future, so it had better at least keep doing so as far as the values of coexistence are concerned.

We survived the first cold war in the nuclear age and we will survive the second in the age of AI, but it will not be thanks to regulation, but because companies find a way to align AI with human values and to put the big red button in the right place on robots.
