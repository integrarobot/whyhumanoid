That robot is not what it seems

Let's talk about the biases that prevent us from appreciating the full potential of humanoid robots.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 2 April 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

We humans love telling stories; we have been doing it since we lived in caves, or on the savannah, which has its positive side, because it has allowed us to get this far. But it also has its negative side, because, as Nassim Taleb explains in his famous book The Black Swan, this tendency to create coherent stories to explain the world, even when those stories do not reflect the true complexity, randomness or uncertainty of events, leads us to overvalue retrospective explanations, simplify complexity and ignore whatever does not fit the narrative.

Moreover, like every cognitive bias, although narrativity has its uses for biological survival, in a world so different from the primitive one it can harm us, because it gives us a false sense of understanding, makes us more vulnerable to rare and unexpected events (the Black Swans) and prevents us from preparing for the improbable but possible.

Now, why am I telling you about narrativity, and what does it have to do with robots? I'll explain below.

I was reading the news that the agency WWP has partnered with NVIDIA, Boston Dynamics and Canon to introduce robotics into its audiovisual content production activity, and the way they explained it made me realize that narrativity, in the case of robotics, has led me to pigeonhole this technology into very specific areas of human activity and to completely rule out others where its usefulness could be enormous.

This is how we arrive at another of the biases Taleb talks about in his books, domain dependence, which explains that our knowledge or skills do not transfer well from one domain to another, especially when we move from a simple or familiar environment to an uncertain, complex or random one. This leads us to make the following mistakes: we confuse competence in a structured environment with competence in real life; we overvalue formal education or theoretical knowledge in domains where uncertainty predominates; and we make mistakes because we apply intuitions or models in the wrong domain (for example, using conventional statistics on phenomena with non-Gaussian distributions).

Reading the WWP news will help you better understand what I mean. It is important to do so, because behind this explanation lies the key to why humanoid robots are going to achieve enormous penetration in all kinds of activities in the coming years.

WPP has been working with Boston Dynamics' Spot robot since 2020, leveraging LiDAR technology to capture entire environments, which is particularly important in virtual production, an increasingly crucial component in the creation of AI-driven advertising content. However, there are limitations in how robotics is currently used in these environments. Robotic arms and platforms can be heavy and their installation takes a lot of time, sometimes up to half a day. Even the smallest adjustments to shots can require dismantling and rebuilding all the equipment, which consumes valuable shooting time. Ensuring the safety and well-being of camera operators is also crucial, reducing their exposure to dangerous locations, extreme conditions and physically demanding tasks. Even in controlled environments, such as shooting luxury commercials, operators often face challenges, such as holding high-angle shots of cars or capturing intricate details of watch parts and fine leather goods. These repetitive positions can be uncomfortable and exhausting. To explore possible solutions to these challenges, WPP collaborated with NVIDIA, Boston Dynamics and Canon to investigate how combinations of the latest technologies could be applied to future production sets. This motivated them to look for other unique use cases and look to the future with new possibilities that could be implemented with Atlas to facilitate production. In this way, the Boston Dynamics robot was placed behind a camera to demonstrate how humanoid robotics could address some of the problems faced on today's sets and elevate human creativity.

And now let's go back to Taleb to explain what this WPP news is trying to tell us:

🗣 ️ First, narrativity: we are so used to thinking of robots in factories that we even find it hard to imagine that they could work doing household chores. We are gradually overcoming this, because manufacturers show us pictures of how these machines can do jobs in a house, but even so, there is still a lot of work to do for people to understand that versatility is one of the great values of this technology.

🕳️ Second, context dependence: we run the risk of not knowing how to extrapolate the capabilities these machines have, because in our heads we do not imagine them doing jobs outside factories. That is why this news, which talks about elevating human creativity, caught my attention. It is not just about delegating to the robot the work a person should not be doing, but about the person being able to devote their time to thinking about how to get the best result from their work.

To finish this article, I invite you to reflect on the implications that the arrival of general-purpose robots driven by the development of Physical AI may have for you. Are you ready for this new technological revolution?

🤔 Have you reflected on the impact this has on your work? A television camera operator, for instance, must think about how to use their experience to explore new ways of doing their job, because soon a machine will do the most mechanical part, so that they will have to devote themselves to the more intellectual part of the matter.

🧭 If you have a company or lead a team, have you thought about how to take advantage of this technology to maximize your impact on the market? Technology keeps offering us new horizons to explore, and you are surely already looking at how to take advantage of GenAI, but bear in mind that robotics will soon arrive to offer other opportunities.

💰 Imagine what new business models will emerge as soon as humanoid robots are on the market. We are talking about ideas such as RaS (Robot as a Service), which will offer opportunities to many companies that design, manufacture or customize robots, but there will also be room for many other business ideas you may come up with.

Bonus: be sure to read the full article on how WPP uses Atlas to boost its activity, as it will greatly open your mind to the future possibilities of humanoid robots.

The usefulness of humanoid robots such as Atlas lies in their design, created to navigate and interact with the human world. Their ability to repeat complex movements allows them to integrate seamlessly into existing workflows and to use equipment such as production cameras in innovative ways. This technology allows us to push the limits of what is possible in content creation, providing unprecedented opportunities to tell captivating stories and present products in previously unimaginable ways.
