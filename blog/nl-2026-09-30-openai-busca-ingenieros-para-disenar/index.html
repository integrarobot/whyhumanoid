OpenAI is looking for engineers to design actuators ⚙️

I wonder whether OpenAI is going to manufacture all the robot's components or do as other manufacturers do and turn to external suppliers.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 30 September 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

What leads a company devoted to developing LLMs to hire an actuator design engineer?

If we read the job offer, it is quite clear that OpenAI wants to design its own actuators for robots:

"We are looking for a Senior Actuator Design and Integration Engineer to lead the development of custom electromechanical actuators for advanced robotic systems"

For me this news came at a time when I am thinking a lot about which part of the humanoid robot manufacturing value chain is going to capture the most business in the coming years.

Until now my conclusion was that robot designers would be the biggest beneficiaries, but I don't want to stop listening to all the people saying that among the bottlenecks of physical AI that can bring the most profit are robot actuators.

My main argument for considering that robot designers, such as Tesla, Agility, Figure, 1X, Apptronik, … will be the ones capturing the most value is initially based on the observation that these companies may have the interest and some capacity to also manufacture the actuators, but this may not be the case, and therefore a lot of business may indeed be generated for the suppliers of these robot components.

In the case of other components, such as batteries, cameras, sensors, … I already see it as more complicated for robot designers to be able to do everything, but with actuators I had my doubts.

That said, what we are going to do next is review what actuators are and how they fit into the development of humanoid robots.

What are actuators?

Robot actuators are responsible for converting energy into movement and force. In a humanoid robot they allow it to bend a knee, lift an arm or close a hand. If we make the comparison with our body, they perform a function similar to that of muscles, although their operation and structure are different.

When we talk about an electric actuator for a humanoid, we are normally talking about a set of parts: a motor, a transmission, sensors, bearings and a housing. The electronics that control the motor can be inside or outside the module. That is why, when a company says it manufactures actuators, it is important to look at exactly what that product includes.

To understand how this assembly works, we can start with the motor, which generates the movement, and continue with the transmission, which adapts it to the needs of each joint, for example through a reducer that transforms the motor's fast rotation into a slower movement but with greater rotational force, what in robotics is called torque. To this are added the sensors, which make it possible to check the position and movement of the joint so that the control system can adjust what the robot is doing.

What types of actuators are there?

Actuators can be classified according to the energy they use or the movement they produce.

By energy, the main families are electric, hydraulic and pneumatic. Electric ones use motors; hydraulic ones, pressurized liquid; and pneumatic ones, compressed air. A well-known example of evolution between technologies is Boston Dynamics' Atlas, which went from its previous hydraulic humanoid to a fully electric one.

By movement, we can distinguish between rotary actuators, which turn a shaft, and linear ones, which push or pull in a straight line. A linear actuator can also bend a knee if it transmits its movement through a mechanism. Likewise, a rotating motor can drive the fingers through cables or tendons.

Within electric ones there is another decision that has a lot to do with business, and that is how the motor is connected to the joint. For this we can use harmonic, planetary or cycloidal reducers, each with different advantages in precision, size, efficiency or impact resistance. There are also direct-drive designs, without a reducer, and quasi-direct-drive designs, known as QDD, which combine a motor capable of generating a lot of torque with a small reduction.

This situation determines the demands on suppliers. If we use a small motor with a high reduction, we give more responsibility to the reducer. If we reduce less, the motor has to provide more torque, and its materials, electronics and heat management become more important. Therefore, two humanoid robots may need different supply chains depending on the engineering decisions the manufacturer makes.

How do actuators relate to the robot's other components?

To understand it, we can think of a robot that has to pick up a box. The cameras and other sensors help locate it. The software calculates how to approach and grasp it. The control system turns that plan into movement and force commands for the actuators. The battery provides the energy and the electronics regulate the current reaching the motors.

While the robot moves, the position, force or contact sensors send information back to the control system so that it keeps correcting the action. This continuous correction makes it possible to adjust a grip or react when the load changes. Therefore, the AI responsible for interpreting a task and the controllers that regulate each joint work at different levels of the system.

In addition, decisions about one component affect the others. For example, if a hand weighs more, moving it may demand more effort from the elbow and shoulder. If the actuators lose too much energy as heat, that affects autonomy and cooling. And if a joint has backlash or responds with delay, the software has to work with those physical limitations.

This helps to understand why a company like OpenAI may want to take part in designing the actuators. One possible explanation is that it wants to adapt the body to the capabilities it intends to develop with its software.

Who manufactures actuators?

Here it is worth distinguishing between companies that sell an integrated actuator and those that manufacture some of its parts. A reducer supplier and a manufacturer of complete modules take part in the same market but offer different products.

Among the suppliers of integrated assemblies we find Schaeffler, which has presented actuators for humanoids, and Laifual, which offers reducers and joint modules. There are also robot manufacturers that sell their own components, as is the case of Unitree.

If we go down to the parts, the map widens. Harmonic Drive and Leaderdrive are references in harmonic reducers. Kollmorgen offers frameless motors that are integrated inside a joint. If we drew up a complete map of suppliers, we would also have to include specialists in screws, encoders, bearings, electronics and magnets. So an actuator designed by the robot manufacturer can bring together parts from several of these companies.

So, to assess the business opportunity, we have to go one step further, since having a product in the catalogue does not prove that a company is supplying large volumes to humanoid manufacturers; it is necessary to know what customers it has, which parts have been validated and how many it can deliver while maintaining quality. That is the point we must evaluate in suppliers: when a technical capability starts to become a business.

Does it make sense for an OEM to manufacture its own actuators?

For the robot manufacturer, usually called an OEM in the industry, controlling the design of the actuators can make it possible to better adjust the weight, force, consumption, noise or response of each joint. It can also facilitate improvements when software and body are developed together.

But here a distinction appears that affects my initial approach: designing your own actuator, assembling it and manufacturing all its parts are different decisions. A company can control the architecture of the assembly and buy the motor, reducers or sensors from specialists. In fact, OpenAI's job offer includes working with suppliers and taking part in their selection and validation.

Manufacturing more components in-house can be attractive if it brings a performance advantage, secures supply or reduces cost with sufficient volume. In exchange, it requires machinery, processes, specialized staff and the capacity to produce thousands of units with consistent quality. Getting a prototype to work solves part of the problem, but repeating it profitably poses another.

Buying actuators from a specialist can make it possible to move faster and take advantage of a factory that spreads its investments across several customers. But it can also create dependence and limit some design decisions. The right combination will depend on which components differentiate the robot, on the supplier's capacity and on the real volume the OEM is going to manufacture.

Therefore, the fact that manufacturers want their own actuators leaves an opportunity open for suppliers. It may even increase demand for specialized components.

The question I want to keep exploring is which part of that actuator OEMs can manufacture competitively and for which part it pays them to work with others. This forces us to refine the idea of who will capture the most value. Controlling the complete robot can give an important commercial position, but a supplier that is hard to replace can keep part of the business. To compare both opportunities we will have to look at margins, the investment required and bargaining power, as well as how many robots are sold.

Going back to OpenAI, the job offer makes clear that it wants to take part in designing the actuators, although we don't yet know for which robot, which makes me think that, to advance in physical AI, it also wants more control over the body that will execute what its software decides.

And to finish, those who are clear that actuators are going to generate a lot of business in themselves are KraneShares, which has launched on the Nasdaq TORK, an ETF focused on manufacturers of actuators and components such as motors, reducers, controllers and sensors.
