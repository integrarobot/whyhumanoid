How are robots put to the test?

Systems for analyzing robots' capabilities will be very useful when the time comes to decide which robot to buy.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 24 June 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

One of the keys to why LLMs are evolving so fast has been the great competition among AI labs to have the best models, which materialized in their position in evaluation rankings. For example, a model developed in Japan called Fugu has just been presented, putting the lab that develops it, Sakana, on the AI map thanks to having positioned itself very well in those LLM evaluation rankings. We have seen something similar before with other labs that are not usually among the first, as was the case with Deepseek and xAI with Grok.

The fact is that a quite similar situation could arise in robotics, but in this case it is not just about evaluating the cognitive capabilities of Physical AI models but also manual dexterity, movement capability and other skills, which makes evaluating robots a much bigger challenge than evaluating Generative AI models.

You should know, for example, that very famous scientists in AI, such as François Chollet, have devoted a large part of their research efforts to establishing the criteria needed to determine whether AI is really intelligent, through evaluation processes such as, in his case, the ARC Prize.

Thus in robotics we are in a situation similar to the one with LLMs 3 years ago, when Google, Anthropic and others began releasing their own models and the competition began to unseat the pioneer of AI chatbots: ChatGPT.

And in robotics we have been seeing for some time different attempts to create these systems for evaluating robot performance; for example, some of the most popular initiatives so far have been:

  • RoboLab-120: consists of a simulation-based performance test with experiments carried out by NVIDIA staff.
  • RoboArena: a distributed, simulation-based robotics ranking where experiments are carried out in reality.
  • MolmoSpaces: an open source platform for robotics development, compatible with multiple simulations and training methods, which offers a leaderboard of mobile robots with a variety of different tasks

And now NIST (the US National Institute of Standards and Technology) has presented a comprehensive method for evaluating the minimum physical capabilities expected of humanoid robots, which aims to be the first performance standard for humanoid robots since the 2015 DARPA Robotics Challenge

"In a decade in which Optimus, Figure, Agility, Apptronik, Unitree and a dozen other humanoid platforms have attracted billions in investment, there is still no agreed way to measure what any of them can actually do. Marketing videos have filled this gap". Aaron Prather, director of the Robotics and Autonomous Systems Program at ASTM International.

NIST's intelligent systems division has made this proposal based on its previous collaboration with the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (DARPA) to evaluate the capabilities of humanoids in industry and academia, and to guide their future development.

NIST proposes this performance reference model as a set of low-impact locomotion and manipulation tasks. To do so, they use previously defined and standardized test methods and performance metrics. These tasks are intended to represent the minimum capabilities of commercially available humanoid robots for industrial, domestic, healthcare and other applications.

The goal is for the results of these tests to establish measurement parameters for the sector's leading robots, as well as a common set of tasks for researchers and manufacturers, providing a performance baseline while the following tests are carried out:

  • Basic, domain-independent mobility and manipulation/dexterity capabilities of humanoid robots.
  • Coordinated capabilities combining locomotion and manipulation tasks.
  • Whole-body awareness and control through manipulation tasks in confined spaces.
  • Minimal reasoning, task and scene understanding, and decision-making.

Another initiative along the same lines in trying to evaluate robots' dexterity is the COMPARE ecosystem, a community backed by the National Science Foundation and focused on robotic manipulation. This ecosystem offers a case study for developing robot tests in which collaborators from industry, academia, government and standards organizations are already taking part.

Simulators, such as NVIDIA's Cosmos, are another useful tool in this robot evaluation challenge; in this case it has five synthetic datasets covering robotics, autonomous driving, physical interactions, digital humans and warehouse operations, and researchers can use them in simulation, generating examples that are scarce, dangerous or hard to test in the physical world.

Why is this important for us?

Soon a time will come when we will have to make important decisions in robotics, because right now the range of possibilities is quite limited. If you dare to go for a humanoid, there is barely the option of Unitree, and if you don't go that far, you can settle for a cobot, a quadruped or any other robot format focused on education. But soon the main robot manufacturers will start putting them on sale, and then many people will have to make highly significant decisions. For example:

  • The company for which buying a single robot will not be enough, because what it really needs is a whole fleet to meet its labour needs. Deciding to invest 50,000 euros in one robot is not the same as multiplying that by 10, 100 or even 1,000.
  • Those who buy a robot not to use it themselves but to offer it to others, whether by selling it, renting it or in any other type of business model, where the initial investment may be significant and making a good decision may be critical for the future of the business.
  • Those who decide to specialize in offering robot-related services, whether software, maintenance, deployment, customization, … will also have to decide which company to marry initially and to what extent to learn about the subject to become real specialists in those robots.

As you can see, the subject of robot testing does not remain a matter for scientists; it is going to have enormous business implications, which is why it is important that we keep an eye on the evolution of this kind of testing system so that, when the time comes to decide which robots to buy, we can take into account the criteria derived from these evaluation systems.
