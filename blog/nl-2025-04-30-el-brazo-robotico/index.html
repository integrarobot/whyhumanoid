🦾 The robotic arm

From surgical robots to the industrial applications of the good old robotic arm ;)

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 30 April 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

General-purpose robots will be a reality one day, just like AGI, but until then there is a lot of good to be gained from the specialized robotic technologies we currently have. Look, for example, at the work surgical robots are doing. I, at least, have always been fascinated by the Da Vinci robot from the successful company Intuitive Surgical, which has had FDA approval to operate since 2000.

Although, of course, here we are not yet talking about robots with Artificial Intelligence, which are the ones now beginning to become a reality thanks to the development of LLMs that give them the cognitive capabilities needed to operate in the real world — never was a word more apt — in this case operating autonomously without needing to be teleoperated by a human.

So, when a technology succeeds and starts to become relatively mature, the normal thing is for competition to arrive, which somewhat upsets the pioneers' plans, until little by little the market puts everyone in their place. In any case, it is interesting to see how Da Vinci is beginning to have competitors, as is the case of Hugo from the major medical technology company Medtronic.

Thus Meditronic presents the Hugo RAS system as a soft-tissue robotic platform for which it expects FDA approval in 2025, so as to drive its entry into the US surgical robotics market. Although we are not yet talking about robots with autonomy, like the humanoids we talk about here every week, something Elon Musk senses will not take long to arrive:

Robots will surpass good human surgeons within a few years and the best human surgeons within about five years. Neuralink had to use a robot to insert the electrodes into the brain and the computer, since it was impossible for a human to achieve the required speed and precision.

As you can imagine, it may be many years before we see an Optimus inside an operating room, which does not mean that we cannot now enjoy the benefits that all the technological advances needed to eventually have surgical robots can bring us. I am talking about precision and consistency. Whether the robot is autonomous or teleoperated, its ability to stick to a previously established plan can be very useful in other areas, such as the manufacture of electronic products.

That said, just as a few weeks ago we paid attention to advances in the development of robot hands, let's now do the same with robotic arms, which are probably the most popular and advanced concept in robotics to date.

The application of robotic arms spans a wide variety of sectors, from manufacturing to space exploration. In the case of industrial robotics, for example, robotic arms have proven very useful for assembly, welding, painting and material handling tasks. This type of robot, like those made by Fanuc or KUKA, is characterized by its precision, speed and ability to repeat a task tirelessly.

In recent years, we have seen how advances in the design of these robotic arms have allowed greater adaptability to more complex and delicate tasks, which brings us ever closer to the concept of a general-purpose robot, once the capabilities of these arms can be fitted into the full body of a humanoid.

A good example of these capabilities is the Reacher robotic arm from Rethink Robotics, which differs from other industrial robots in that it has an interface that allows it to be quickly programmed to perform new tasks without the need for extensive technical training.

So let's look at Reacher to understand a little better the state of development and implementation of robotic arms in companies:

Reacher has been designed to interact in a more accessible and flexible way with human work environments. Its main features are:

✍️ Collaborative design: it has been designed with the aim of working side by side with humans, which means it is intended to be safe and efficient in a shared environment. Unlike other industrial robotic arms, which are usually separated from operators by safety barriers because of their fast movements and great weight, Reacher is a flexible, lightweight cobot, ideal for collaborating directly with humans.

👁️ Sensor and vision technology: one of the most interesting aspects of Reacher is that it incorporates advanced sensor and vision technology to perceive its environment. This allows it to work intuitively and adapt to changes in its environment without the need for complex programming. In addition, safety sensors ensure the robot can operate safely near humans, detecting possible collisions or dangerous situations.

⌨️ Simple programming: Reacher stands out for its simple programming interface. In general, Rethink Robotics robots are designed to lower the barrier to entry so that users without great robotics experience can program and operate the robot easily. This is possible thanks to a system based on visual programming software, which allows the robot to be quickly configured for various tasks without the need for complicated code.

🏭 Applications: although Reacher is not as focused on high-precision manufacturing as larger industrial robots, it is ideal for tasks in environments where flexibility and collaboration are important. Some of its applications include: logistics, for material handling and storage in warehouse environments; assembly, to replace humans in repetitive, low-risk, high-volume tasks; testing and experimentation, in laboratories or educational environments, where Reacher can be used to perform tasks that require precision but do not need mass automation.

As you have seen, the development of robotic arms has advanced a great deal in incorporating perception capabilities through tactile sensors, computer vision and feedback technology, which allows them to adapt their behaviour according to environmental conditions.

Beyond this specific case of Rethink Robotics' Reacher, we find applications of this technology in all kinds of situations and for all kinds of purposes, as is the case of the robotic arm developed by NASA for space missions as part of the Robonaut project, which is not limited to performing mechanical tasks but also integrates a sensor system to detect contact with objects, making it more precise when interacting with the environment. This type of technology is also beginning to reach more everyday applications, such as the field of assistive robotics.

Currently, advances in the field of low-cost robotic arms are also significant. Companies such as Open Bionics are facilitating access to robotic prosthetic arms for people with amputations, using lightweight materials, open design and accessible manufacturing processes such as 3D printing. These advances are paving the way for future developments in more accessible and functional robotic arms for everyday life.

Another of the most interesting developments is the evolution of soft robotic arms. These arms are made of flexible and adaptive materials that allow greater agility and versatility when interacting with fragile objects, such as food or delicate tools. Companies such as Festo have made notable progress in creating robotic arms that imitate the dexterity and softness of organic arms thanks to soft robotics and pneumatic actuators.

And what does the future hold for robotic arms?

There is no doubt that, as we advance in the development of artificial intelligence and materials science, we will see a significant improvement in the autonomy of robotic arms. In addition, cognitive control will allow a robotic arm not only to perform tasks autonomously, but also to understand context and adapt to it intuitively. For example, in surgery, a robotic arm could adjust its movement not only based on pre-programmed data, but also taking into account the patient's actual condition during the operation.

Therefore, although we are far from having fully autonomous robots like the humanoids we talk about here, we are already seeing how robotic arm systems can perform incredibly precise and complex tasks. As in the case of surgical robots, the ability to perform tasks with precision and consistency is one of the greatest advantages robots offer, and this can only get better as technology advances.

And to finish, before leaving you with the featured robotics and Artificial Intelligence news, here is another novelty from Hugging Face, which keeps showing us its great interest in physical AI. It is the SO-101, a 3D-printable robotic arm developed through the Le Robot initiative with AI developers in mind.
