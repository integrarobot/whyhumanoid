The price of robots

There are already those who dare to say that a humanoid robot will cost as much as a household appliance. Here we think the price will be more like that of a car.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 28 May 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

In an interview with Luis Sentis, scientific advisor and co-founder of Apptronik, he surprised us with the statement that humanoid robots for the home will come to cost 1,500 dollars and industrial ones 5,000 dollars.

This is the first time I have heard such a low price, comparable to that of a household appliance rather than a car. And Sentis's argument is that, in the end, what makes a car expensive is the amount of metal used to manufacture it, so since the robot is much smaller its cost will be much lower.

It is true that equating the price of a humanoid robot with that of a household appliance would greatly simplify the market's perception of how widely this technology will be adopted, but I think the question here is not whether people will want to have a machine capable of taking care of the tedious household chores, but whether robot manufacturers will be able to give them the skills needed to do so with sufficient quality. That is, it is not so much about hardware and price, but about software.

On the other hand, the problem we face in getting the price of robots to become as low as that of a household appliance rather than as high as that of a car is the amortization of the R&D investment robotics companies are having to make to bring their products to market. When there are still hardly any humanoid robots on sale and none of them is really capable of performing the functions on which we have pinned our expectations, it is easy to think that affordable pricing may take many more years, as happened, for example, with Tesla cars, where the higher-cost models, or models for the rich, made it possible to finance the manufacture of utility models accessible to a greater number of people.

Then there is the question of timing, because it is the question everyone asks me. Are we talking about the near or distant future? Less than 10 years or much more? This is where Elon Musk has once again stuck his neck out, saying that in 4 or 5 years humanoid robots will have a significant effect on the gross domestic product of some country. Having said that, we have to consider to what extent this is a real expectation based on his knowledge of how the development of Optimus is progressing, or whether he is simply once again putting into his team's heads a date by which he would like to start seeing the results of all the engineering work they are doing.

Finally, we have to discuss the issue of competition, because unlike what happened with electric cars, where for a few years Tesla had very few competitors, which allowed it to achieve significant market dominance until the arrival of Chinese companies, in the case of Humanoid Robots it is very noticeable that competition is going to be enormous.

We are talking about more than 60 companies that we have located, to which many others would have to be added, especially from China, which for now remain under the radar or which we have not yet discovered. This means that prices may be significantly affected by this competition, especially if it comes from Asia, where they have clearly chosen to make the technology accessible first, in order to then move on to the monetization stage.
