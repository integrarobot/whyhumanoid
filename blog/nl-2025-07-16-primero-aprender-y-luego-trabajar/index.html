First learn, then work

There is much debate about the state of the art of robotics, but one thing is clear: every day that passes brings important improvements.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 16 July 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Just a week ago Hugging Face put the Reachy mini robot on sale, the result of the recent acquisition of the company Pollen Robotics, and they have already sold units worth 1 million dollars. The price of the robot is 299 dollars for the basic version and 449 for the wireless version, which implies they must have sold some 3,000 units.

Reachy Mini is advertised as the open source robot for the AI developers of today and of the future, which shows that what they are looking for is mainly educational use, with the aim that the people who buy it contribute to driving the development of robotics.

Reachy is certainly not the first educational robot born with this purpose; over the last few years we have seen many similar initiatives appear, but probably none had foundations as solid as having the full support of Hugging Face. It is hard to say how much of the current success of generative AI is due to this open source community, but its contribution is undoubtedly tremendously important.

Why is this important?

Having thousands of new developers experimenting in robotics will give this sector a new boost, as has already happened with other initiatives, but in this case it may become even more popular and attract many more people wanting to join this new gold rush that is going to be represented by Humanoid Robots.

Forgive the gold rush terminology, but it is handy for referring to what Jim Fan, NVIDIA's director of robotics, recently said, referring to this sector as the wonderful wild west. This makes one think that, if the beginnings of the United States had that tumultuous stage before reaching the later phase of prosperity, something similar may be happening with robots.

In fact, Jim refers to GPT1 and Bert to situate the current state of robotics. If you were one of those who tried those Generative AI tools back in 2018, you can tell us whether they resembled in any way what the presentation of ChatGPT meant 4 years later. So what this technology phenomenon is trying to show us is that Humanoid Robots still have a few years of crossing the desert ahead of them.

I believe robotics' GPT-1 is already on Arxiv, but we don't know exactly which one. It could be world models, machine learning, learning from human video, sim2real, real2sim, etc., or any combination of them. The debates are intense. Entropy is through the roof. The nature of robotics also greatly complicates the design space. Unlike the clean world of bits for text strings (LLMs), we roboticists have to deal with the messy world of atoms. After all, there is a piece of software-defined metal in the loop. LLM experts may find it hard to believe, but so far roboticists have not yet agreed on a benchmark. Robots have different capabilities: some are better at acrobatics, while others at object manipulation. Some are designed for industrial use, while others are devoted to household tasks. Body-mind integration is not just a research novelty, but an essential feature for a universal robotic brain. The best time to get in is when consensus peaks. We are still at the beginning of a loss curve: there are strong signs of life, but we are very far from convergence. Every step takes us into the unknown. But of one thing I am sure: there is no AGI without touching, feeling and being embedded in this chaotic world.

Revealing words that lead me to think about the importance of learning: basically, we still do not know the best way to train robots so that they learn to get by in the real world, and that is why not a week goes by without news of a new Artificial Intelligence model that aims to solve this problem.

For example, here are some news items and articles I had collected for this week that tell us about the state of the art of technology development for robot training:

1️⃣ The Toyota Research Institute published this week the results of its study on Large Behavior Models (LBMs) that can be used to train general-purpose robots. The study showed that a single LBM can learn hundreds of tasks and use prior knowledge to acquire new skills with 80% less training data.

2️⃣ Robobrain is an open source embodied brain model designed to unify perception, reasoning and planning for complex robot tasks in physical environments. It features a heterogeneous architecture with a vision encoder and a language model, which allows it to achieve solid performance across a broad spectrum of embodied reasoning tasks

3️⃣ Generative Value Learning (GVL) is a new robot training technique that solves the problem of knowing whether the robot is on the right track to complete a task. They achieved this simply by showing the robot a series of images and videos of humans, without needing to train the system for each task.

4️⃣ What are world models? This interesting article by Chris Paxton, who works at Agility Robotics, shows us the current challenges faced by developers of artificial intelligence systems that allow robots to imagine or predict possible futures from their current state and the actions they can take next.

I'll end this article by bringing up again the topic of the ChatGPT moment of robotics, because through an article by Paritosh Mohan we can better understand what stage of robotics development we are at and what challenges remain to be solved.

Where are we?

👣 Autonomy 1.0: handcrafted robotics (before 2015): robots with fixed rules and classical vision. No learning at all. They only worked in highly controlled environments. E.g.: Roomba, Kiva, DARPA Challenge.

🤖 Autonomy 2.0: AI modules (2015–2022): deep learning is introduced into vision, but the rest of the system (planning, control…) remains manual. E.g.: Tesla, Waymo, Spot.

🌐 Autonomy 3.0: foundation models (since 2023): transformer-type models that understand video, language and sensors. They can learn new tasks and explain themselves. E.g.: RT-2, GROOT, OpenVLA.

🤝 Autonomy 4.0: full collaboration (future): robots that cooperate with each other and with humans. They share information, adapt on the fly and understand our intentions. E.g.: swarms, assistive robots, real-time logistics.

What comes next?

A truly versatile robot, capable of moving from a warehouse to a kitchen or a construction site without having to be completely retrained, is still about 10 years away. To reach real Autonomy 3.0, several key challenges in intelligence, hardware, operations and safety must be solved.

🧠 Better memory and reasoning

Problem: current models forget quickly and do not reason over the long term. Goal: robots that keep goals and maps of the environment in their memory for minutes and that remember what is there even when it is out of sight.

🔁 Learning by doing

Problem: they struggle to adapt to the real world. Goal: teach the robot through imitation + trial and error directly in real situations, without depending so much on simulators.

⚡ Efficient inference

Problem: they do not achieve a good balance between speed, precision and energy consumption. Goal: lightweight models that combine video, world and action in a single step, optimized for speed and energy efficiency.

🎮 Collecting data at scale

Problem: getting good robot data is expensive and slow. Goal: teleoperation tools that collect hours of useful data per shift, with variety and realism to scale learning.

🧪 Simulation ≠ Reality

Problem: what they learn in simulation fails when used in the real world. Goal: simulators that model physical contacts well and reduce the gap between the virtual and the real to less than 10% error.

📱 More affordable hardware

Problem: hardware is slow to improve and too expensive. Goal: cheap actuators, longer-lasting batteries and faster improvement cycles, like those we see in smartphones.

✋ Precise manipulation

Problem: bad at delicate tasks. Goal: precise tactile control so they can do fine work such as screwing in screws or handling fragile objects.

🚨 Active and guaranteed safety

Problem: there are no real guarantees that robots will not cause harm. Goal: safety systems that remain active at all times, with clear limits on speed, force and space.

The path is marked out; now we just have to travel it.
