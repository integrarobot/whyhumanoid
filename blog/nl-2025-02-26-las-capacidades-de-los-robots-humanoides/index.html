The capabilities of humanoid robots

What about voice? Why don't humanoid robots talk? We are going to analyze it in this article.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 26 February 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

Last week there were important developments in the field of robotics driven by Artificial Intelligence; especially regarding humanoid robots, we should highlight the presentation of Figure's Helix software and the new video demonstrating the home butler skills of 1X's Neo robot.

In both cases there is a dominant pattern: the demonstration of capabilities focuses heavily on manual skill rather than cognitive skill; in fact, none of these robots talks, although they do receive voice instructions.

Let's start with Helix, the Artificial Intelligence software developed by Figure after it announced that it was ending its work with OpenAI (shareholders in the company) in order to develop its own software in-house and have that autonomy that is so necessary when you want to have everything under control without depending on third parties.

Helix is a vision-language-action (VLA) foundation model for controlling general-purpose humanoid robots. That is, to the multimodal Artificial Intelligence models we are used to, for example when we upload an image to ChatGPT for it to analyze or point the camera with Gemini so that it interprets what we are seeing, in this case we add the ability to take action, but generating movement rather than content.

The basis on which Helix has been developed lies in replicating how the language and image models we use for content generation work: they do not need to have been trained specifically for a task, but have capabilities to generalize or deduce what they should do from previous experience in another domain.

Therefore, thanks to Helix it is not necessary to program each of the actions a robot must perform in the home, or to train it specifically for them with images of that activity; based on generic training, the robot can extrapolate certain actions to others it has never seen before. This is very important specifically for work in the home, where, unlike in industry, there are usually no standards or optimized processes: every house is a world of its own and is organized very differently from another.

Thinking fast, thinking slow

Image: Image

Have you heard of System 1 and System 2, proposed as a theory of human thought by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky? Surely the people at Figure who created Helix know it and must have been inspired by it to design the control of the robot's upper body.

But this is not new: the reasoning models used in Generative Artificial Intelligence, such as ChatGPT's o1, are also based on this idea, making the machine think step by step or create a chain of thought from which to articulate its reasoning. It is the folk wisdom of "*dress me slowly, I'm in a hurry*" reinterpreted by high engineering.

In this way, the Helix model introduces an innovative approach in humanoid robotics by dividing its decision-making process into two complementary systems: System 1 and System 2. Inspired by the theory of how we humans process information, it proposes a set made up of a fast, intuitive system and a slow, analytical system. In the case of Helix, this division allows robots to combine precision and speed with reasoning and adaptability.

💪 S1: Fast, reactive control

Helix's System 1 is responsible for executing the robot's physical movements quickly and precisely. It runs at 200 Hz, which means it makes decisions every 5 milliseconds. Its main task is executing motor actions in real time, continuously adjusting the position of the fingers, wrist and torso to perform smooth and coordinated movements.

  • It uses an 80-million-parameter neural network based on transformers that allows it to generate movements in real time.
  • It runs on a separate GPU, optimized to perform motion control computations efficiently.
  • It is responsible for translating high-level instructions into precise physical actions, such as grasping an object with the right force or adjusting the robot's posture according to the environment.

S1 does not make complex decisions or interpret the overall context; it responds quickly to the information it receives from S2.

🧠 S2: Semantic reasoning and planning

Helix's System 2 is responsible for interpreting the environment and language and for planning actions. It operates at a lower frequency of 7-9 Hz, since its task is not immediate execution but understanding and strategic decision-making.

  • It uses a pre-trained vision-language model (VLM) with 7 billion parameters, which allows it to interpret images, understand natural language commands and plan actions.
  • It receives visual information from the robot's cameras and from the state of its joints.
  • It translates text commands and its perception of the environment into a latent vector that synthesizes the intention of the action.

For example, if the robot receives the command "*pick up the butter and hand it to the robot on the left*", S2 analyzes the meaning of the sentence, identifies the butter in the image and decides what sequence of movements is needed to complete the task.

This decoupled design of Helix allows both systems to operate in parallel, each in its own optimal time frame:

  • S2 receives the natural language command, analyzes the scene and generates an action plan.
  • S1 receives the latent vector from S2 and translates that information into continuous movements adjusted in real time.
  • S2 keeps monitoring the scene, adjusting the plan if conditions change (for example, if the object moves or if the other robot changes its position).

Thanks to this, robots are fast and precise in execution, but also adaptive and intelligent in decision-making, solving one of the current problems of robotics: the need to combine reactive motor control with generalist reasoning.

Until now we knew very little about how the brains of robots were going to work, especially as far as the action part is concerned, and now, thanks to the presentation of Helix, we have much more information about it. I am sure this will set a trend for many other companies that design and manufacture humanoid robots.

🖇️ From LLMs to VLAs

After the invention of reinforcement learning and of Transformers, the type of neural network that has given rise to the entire development of generative Artificial Intelligence, specifically through the Large Language Models (LLMs) that power intelligent assistants or chatbots such as ChatGPT, we are now entering a new stage of development with the arrival of VLAs, these new models that combine vision, language and action.

Vision is necessary to recognize the environment, language to understand the instructions it receives through people's voices, and action to act accordingly based on the instructions it receives.

This combination of elements is the key for the robot to be fully functional, but it has a weak point: the action part is the one for which the least data is available, and it is one of the issues on which most work is being done to eliminate this bottleneck, for example, as we saw in previous weeks, with the initiatives PARTNR from Meta and ARMADA from Apple, to which we must now add MAGMA from Microsoft.

As you can imagine, when an entire industry coordinates to face a common challenge, as has happened before with computing, the Internet and Artificial Intelligence, the normal outcome is that forecasts about the usefulness and impact of humanoid robotics end up being fulfilled.

An example of this is the presentation of the new robot from the company 1X, yes, the other startup backed by OpenAI both financially and technologically.

Image: Image

Neo Gamma is a robot designed specifically to provide services in the home and to do so in a way that is very friendly to human beings, to the point that its aesthetics seem to have been created by IKEA designers. But as you can see in the presentation video, the entire display of capabilities is based on manual work; the robot has hardly any social interaction beyond head and hand movements.

👄 What about voice? Why don't humanoid robots talk?

When you dig into the strategy of the different humanoid robot manufacturers, one of the most striking things is that the vast majority of these robots do not talk, and after thinking about it, I thought I would ask Artificial Intelligence about the reasons that could be behind this situation.

If you look at the robots' demonstration videos, each of them emphasizes certain capabilities: there are those that do pirouettes, like Boston Dynamics' Atlas; those able to display great manual skill, like Tesla's Optimus; those able to carry objects, like Apptronik's Apollo and Agility's Digit; and those able to collaborate with other robots, like Figure's 02; but we find almost no examples of robots that interact through voice.

Well, actually, I'm lying, because some robots do talk, and in fact it is these robots we are going to talk about to finish this article, since most of the capabilities we have been discussing so far could be classified as physical, but there are other capabilities, the social ones, which will need to be looked at later.

Because social robotics is a world within a broader one, which is robotics driven by Artificial Intelligence; in this case we are talking about robots capable of expressing themselves through voice and non-verbal communication. And here we find proposals such as Ameca from Engineered Arts and Aria from Realbotix.

What is going on with these robots? First of all, we have to mention the trade-off involved in focusing on the social interaction part, which means these companies have much less developed physical capabilities in their robots. But it is also worth reflecting on the feeling we get when we see these machines in which there has been an attempt to replicate the appearance of people, but where the result still leaves a lot of room for improvement. And the same happens with voice.

This is where we need to bring back the concept of the uncanny valley; that is, companies that manufacture humanoid robots, specifically those not specifically devoted to social robotics, must be very careful not to generate unrealistic expectations about the capabilities of their robots. At the moment, especially if they focus on physical, manual and intellectual capabilities, making robots talk or look more human can generate disappointment or even fear in humans, which may affect market perception and support for the development of this technology.

For the rest, we would have to bear in mind that, due to battery limitations, these robots will not for now be able to move at high speeds or lift very heavy objects, nor will they be able to display great intellectual capabilities, because all of this consumes energy and greatly limits their operating range.
