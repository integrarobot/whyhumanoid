What Optimus's new hand can teach us 🖐️

Will Tesla achieve a manipulation milestone similar to the one Honor reached for locomotion?

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 22 April 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

"Those who don't run, fly" may be a good way to represent what is happening with Physical AI technology and humanoid robots.

Today I'm going to use two very topical pieces of news to illustrate it.

  1. The Beijing humanoid robot half marathon.
  2. Optimus's new hand.

On 19 April 2026, more than 100 Chinese-made humanoid robots ran alongside 12,000 human runners on a 21-kilometre course in Beijing's E-Town district. What happened over those 21 kilometres can be described as amazing, chaotic and even hilarious all at once, but above all we must acknowledge that it is another of those milestones showing us the future of robotics.

Lightning is the name of the robot that won the race; its manufacturer is Honor, and it completed the course in 50 minutes and 26 seconds, smashing the half marathon world record of 57 minutes, set by Ugandan Jacob Kiplimo, by almost seven minutes. Honor not only won the race, but its robots swept the podium, taking first, second and third place.

From the race, a couple of aspects stand out that tell us how quickly this technology is evolving and improving:

  1. The winning robot of the same race last year finished in 2 hours and 40 minutes. Honor's Lightning completed the same course in 50 minutes. This indicates that we are entering full exponentiality.
  2. In this year's race 40% of the robots ran autonomously, compared with the previous year's race, where the percentage of teleoperation was much higher. This is very important because it tells us how software is improving, not just hardware.

Well, now that we know robots run like the wind and that Honor is going all in on humanoids, let's look at the new hand Tesla has designed for Optimus.

What Tesla is telling us with this new patent is that it is very serious about Optimus. Yes, it sounds strange, but looking at other companies' demos it might seem that a robot that just does things is enough for us. For example, in most Physical AI companies' demos, what we see are robotic arms with grippers that can manipulate all kinds of objects, apparently even quite precisely. But Tesla is thinking about something else.

The level of excellence Tesla is seeking with its robot is another story. If you have tried their cars, you'll know what I mean. And if they achieve something similar, we will be looking at a technology that will leave nobody indifferent.

What I'm talking about is reinventing the concept of the robot. It is about comparing what other manufacturers are doing, which is seeking to offer the basics to handle the everyday activities that can be entrusted to the robot, with something totally different that seeks to go beyond immediate usefulness and even surpass human capabilities.

As an analogy, any robot's hand would be like mine, rather clumsy for most manual work, whereas Optimus's hand aspires to have dexterity equivalent to a surgeon's.

I think this justifies the manufacturing delays of Tesla's robot. I don't like it being this way because I'd like to be able to buy the robot right now, but the difference in the final quality of the product will make the wait worthwhile.

Will Tesla achieve a manipulation milestone similar to the one Honor reached for locomotion?

Having fast, flexible and strong robots is fine, but above all we want skilful robots, and that is what the new Tesla Optimus hand patent conveys.

A patent that describes a mechanical architecture quite different from what is usually seen in most current robots, which tend to be relatively simple. Basically a gripper or a system with few degrees of freedom that can grasp large objects but has considerable limitations when it comes to manipulating small or delicate things.

So Tesla is trying to solve that problem with a design closer to human anatomy.

The central idea of the patent is elegant from an engineering point of view: the motors that generate the movement are not inside the hand, but in the forearm. From there, movement is transmitted to the fingers through cables that work similarly to the tendons of the human arm.

This detail has several important consequences.

  1. Lightness: in robotics there is a principle showing that the more weight you place at the end of a joint, the harder it is to control movement precisely. By moving the actuators to the forearm, mass in the hand is reduced and fine control improves.
  2. Mechanical density: a human hand has many degrees of freedom, and replicating that complexity in a small volume is extremely difficult. Using cables makes it possible to introduce multiple joints in the fingers without having to put motors in each phalanx.
  3. Precision: the cables run inside the palm and wrist through channels and pulleys designed to reduce friction and maintain the right tension. This allows the force applied by each finger to be controlled more delicately.

So if we look closely at the design shown in the patent, the goal seems clear. It is not just about grasping objects, but about manipulating them with almost human dexterity. And this brings incalculable value because it expands the possibilities of where and how Optimus can work in all kinds of situations and places (the Moon and Mars included)
