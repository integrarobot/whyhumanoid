How Ginkgo is robotizing biology

Can cells be manufactured the way chips are manufactured?

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 25 February 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Today I want to tell you about a company with a breathtaking story. It is Ginko Bioworks, whose goal is to become the TSMC of biotechnology, for which, instead of manufacturing programmable chips, what it does is manufacture cells programmed to perform a specific new function.

In this way, Ginko works in the field of synthetic biology: designing cells that fulfil a specific function, such as bacteria optimized to produce therapeutic proteins, yeasts modified to manufacture complex molecules used in drugs, and cells designed to produce specific enzymes used in vaccines or therapies.

As I was saying, Ginko's story is breathtaking, but at the same time quite controversial, and not only because of how ambitious its mission is, but because of the ups and downs it has had since its founding as an MIT spin-off in 2028.

For example, among its main milestones, starting from the end: in 2026, the collaboration with OpenAI to use the intelligence of GPT-5 in its autonomous laboratory, so that it can propose experiments, run them at large scale, learn from the results and decide what to test next, thanks to which the cost of protein production can be reduced by 40%.

And if we go much further back, we find news as interesting as the fact that in 2014 Ginko was the first biotech company to take part in the prestigious startup accelerator programme Y Combinator, in which mostly digital projects take part, which shows that the company has long had the goal of uniting biology with bits.

Later, in 2018, the company became a unicorn when it reached a valuation of 1 billion dollars in one of its investment rounds. In 2019 it came to be worth 4.2 billion, and in 2021 it went public through a merger with a SPAC at a valuation of about 20 billion dollars, which positioned it as a dizzying success, at least from a financial point of view.

Thanks to that success, between 2017 and 2022 the company acquired 7 startups, incorporating their most important biotechnology capabilities and innovations.

But this is where things start to go wrong, because a few months after starting to trade on the stock exchange, the company was accused of being a fraud because it counted as revenue the contracts it had with the startups it invested in, which accounted for a significant part of its business volume.

That is when the company began its particular ordeal, because its share price plummeted more than 95%, and it currently has a valuation of about 500 million dollars.

But anyway, we haven't come here to talk about investments but about technology, and that is what we are going to look at next, because after sorting out its financial problems, the company has kept reaching agreements to drive its strategy of building robotized laboratories, and if we look at its collaborations with the pharmaceutical industry, it seems very clear that what matters is continuing with its mission of becoming the TSMC of biotechnology.

  • Ginkgo and Bayer are working on developing biological solutions for agriculture (nitrogen-fixing microbes and biological agricultural technologies).
  • Ginkgo and Pfizer have a collaboration agreement focused on discovering possible RNA-based medicines.
  • Ginkgo and Boehringer Ingelheim are working with a metagenomic database to accelerate the discovery of new therapeutic molecules.
  • Ginkgo and Merck have an agreement to design enzymes for use as biocatalysts in efforts to manufacture pharmaceutical ingredients.
  • Ginkgo and Solvay are working on sustainable biopolymers, expanding R&I capabilities in chemistry and synthetic biology.

And now for the important part, robotic laboratories: what they are and what they are used for:

When we talk about Ginkgo's robotic laboratories, we are referring to an automated infrastructure where biology is treated as a programmable industrial process and where robotic arms do most of the manual work that until now was done by people.

A robotic laboratory is, in essence, a factory of experiments.

Its function is not to execute thousands or millions of variations of a biological design systematically, measure the results and feed that data to computational models that decide what to test next.

The scheme followed to feed this process is as follows:

  • Digital design
  • Automated construction
  • Massive testing
  • Standardized measurement
  • Algorithmic learning

And the cycle repeats continuously.

If we look at it through the prism of robotics, it is a closed perception-decision-action system applied to cells instead of electric motors.

Therefore, Ginko's laboratories are automated platforms that handle substances, culture microorganisms, sequence results and generate structured data. Everything is designed to minimize human intervention and maximize reproducibility, with the aim of being able to scale the process.

In robotics we talk a lot about data, simulation, world models and large-scale training. Ginkgo is trying to do something equivalent in biology: turn every experiment into reusable data that improves the next one.

In this way, if you are able to turn biology into iterative engineering supported by automation and AI models, the speed of discovery increases exponentially.

In short, Ginko offers biotechnology and pharmaceutical companies the hardware and software infrastructure needed to develop their products, so that these companies only have to devote themselves to designing them, just as NVIDIA designs the GPUs that TSMC then manufactures.

Undoubtedly a company that cannot leave us indifferent, because of how ambitious its mission is but also because of how controversial its track record has been so far.

We will keep an eye on its evolution, because what it aims to achieve could change the world or remain just a clever analogy: the TSMC of biology.
