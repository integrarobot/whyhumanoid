OpenClaw wants to get into robots

The trendy AI can contribute a lot to improving robots' autonomy

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 4 February 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

In recent weeks we have been seeing an important advance in AI development, not so much because of the invention of new models or training techniques, but because of the appearance of a new product that solves some of the problems AI Assistants have, which mean we cannot yet fully consider them Agents.

As I suppose you know, both ChatGPT and Claude have been gradually incorporating features that bring them closer to this idea of AI that acts with autonomy, without us having to give all the instructions about the work it has to do, so that what an agent receives is a goal, and it has the ability to execute it as a whole. For this, the agent must be able to interact with the Internet without our supervision and to execute actions in the software on our own computer.

To understand it with an example: when we use deep research or create applications in the canvas, what we are doing is stating a goal, not listing a series of tasks, since it is the AI itself that takes care of defining what needs to be done to satisfy the need we have expressed.

But AI assistants have so far had several problems that OpenClaw, the trendy initiative in the technology world, comes to solve. Namely:

  1. It has persistent memory. That is, it is able to remember the conversations we have throughout the day and connect them to better understand our needs, so that all those interactions enrich one another. For example, if you discussed a topic some time ago and now come back to it, it does not start from scratch, as with any other chatbot; instead it incorporates the conclusions reached previously.
  2. It is proactive. Which means it doesn't wait for you to ask it for things in order to help you; as it gets to know your interests and needs, it is able to propose things that can help you in your day-to-day life or in the work you are doing. This can be done thanks to persistent memory and its ability to connect with the rest of your digital life, which is the next point.
  3. It can control other software. If you give it access, it can use the software on your computer or browse the Internet to have the tools it needs to accomplish the goal you have set for it. This is the most controversial part of all this, because of the security problems it entails. But it is also super useful, because it can become a real virtual assistant when it is able to manage your email, calendar, files, … to take many low-value routine tasks off your hands.

In short, OpenClaw comes to offer us many of the utilities we miss when we use ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini, although as you can imagine it is not the ideal solution either, first because of the security compromise, which means having to be much more careful with what we do, as well as the cost of having an AI working for you all the time, which will affect the licence you use for the AI model the agent runs on.

But anyway, today we haven't come to talk only about AI; as you can imagine, this may have important implications for robotics, and the reason is simple: we need intelligent agents to have humanoid robots.

In other words, to have fully functional robots, some aspects of AI still need to be improved, such as that ability to chain tasks without our having to give them all the instructions. Here reasoning models and VLAs have a lot to contribute, but there is still work to be done on world models, which allow the robot to anticipate the consequences of its actions once it is performing an action in the real world.

And this is where the connection between AI Agents and robotics comes in, because if we manage to get the robot itself to be able to turn to different digital tools to extract the context needed to do a job, it will not be necessary to give it in training all the information about how to do every task it might come to perform.

I sense this is the reason why Brett Adcock, founder of Figure, is also getting into the AI agent business. And why Elon Musk has been investing for so long in everything to do with AI, first as an investor in DeepMind, then as a promoter of OpenAI and now as founder of xAI.

In short, the proactivity that AI applications like OpenClaw can develop will be very useful for having robots with enough autonomy to carry out complete jobs, not just isolated tasks. And this is precisely what Natalie Yeo is seeking to achieve as one of the first people to set out to install OpenClaw on a small robot, and later to try it also with a Unitree G1 robot.

Perhaps it is still early to put forward the idea that these new AI initiatives will give robots greater autonomy, but there is no doubt that people are willing to try, because if we increasingly feel in need of AI support in the digital sphere, when we take that into the real world the need is even greater.

By the way, robotics initiatives by AI labs continue to increase. Some time ago we saw that Anthropic was already experimenting with robot training, and now it is Mistral, the leader in LLMs in Europe, that has also started working in this sector.
