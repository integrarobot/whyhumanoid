🏭 What does it take to scale the manufacture of humanoid robots?

The next big challenge in the development of humanoid robotics is building factories to create robots by the millions

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 17 September 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

"*We have begun construction of our first manufacturing cell in downtown San Francisco, capable of producing hundreds of robots per year. Next year, our goal will be to scale these cells into a larger factory to produce more than 10,000 robots in 2026*".

So says Sankaet Pathak, CEO of Foundation, one of the most promising humanoid robotics companies, but one that is at a very early stage of development. His message is probably a wake-up call for the investors he will need to go from producing hundreds to thousands of robots.

Brett Adcock, CEO of Figure, has already gone through that stage; yesterday he announced that he had closed a 1 billion dollar investment round at a valuation of 39B dollars. Money that comes from Brookfield, NVIDIA, Macquarie, Intel, Align, Tamarack, LG, Salesforce, T-Mobile and Qualcomm.

This fortune is the financial boost the company needs to strengthen the development of Helix, its AI technology that governs how the robots work, and of BotQ, the factory where these machines are built.

[Video embedded in the original article]

"*BotQ is a high-volume manufacturing plant with initial production lines capable of producing up to 12,000 humanoids a year. From now on, the production volume of our robots will continue to grow considerably. We have outlined the plan describing our complete redesign of the robotic architecture, which includes supply chain integration and the introduction of new enterprise software for scalability*".

So large-scale robot manufacturing is what we are going to focus on today, looking at how there are companies at different levels of scaling their operations: we could say that Foundation has already left the lab to start manufacturing hundreds of robots in a small factory, and Figure has already gone through that process, so it now has the factory in which to build thousands of robots.

And this stage of thousands of robots is where Tesla was until it decided to change plans. I am referring to the hardware side, because, as we know, software can be updated from the cloud, so it follows a different pace of development.

And it is on the hardware side that Tesla must have run into problems that have led it to change its manufacturing plans. Initially, the plan was to begin mass production of Optimus 2.0, but now they have decided to wait until 3.0 is designed so that this is the one they manufacture by the thousands.

Elon Musk makes the reason clear in a series of messages on X that give food for thought about how difficult it will be for many humanoid robot manufacturers with no experience in scaling manufacturing operations to prosper:

"*Designing dexterous hands for robots is a very hard problem. Then figuring out how to manufacture them at scale is 100 times harder. Production at scale is 100 times harder than making a prototype. There is no movie that illustrates this. Optimus is a hard problem in engineering, design and especially in manufacturing.*"

Why is Elon placing so much emphasis on how hard it is to produce robots at scale?

This is what Grok answers on the subject: Elon Musk has been emphasizing the challenges of producing humanoid robots at scale, particularly with Tesla's Optimus, due to a combination of technical, economic and strategic factors that reflect both the realities of engineering and his ambitions for the technology's transformative impact.

It seems a very generic answer, but when you ask the AI to go into depth, things change quite a bit, because it shows us a fairly realistic picture of the challenge involved in making humanoid robot manufacturers' plans come true.

So let's look at what those challenges are, to better understand what the future of this industry will be like, an industry in which we have placed so many expectations but which is still at such an early stage that nobody can say it will be a bed of roses:

1️⃣ Unprecedented technical complexity: producing Optimus is an extremely difficult engineering and manufacturing problem, even more so than designing prototypes. Improving the robot's design to make it easier to manufacture and managing a complex supply chain is by far the hardest part. Components such as dexterous hands are especially complicated, and scaling them to mass production is even harder. This reflects the need to integrate thousands of sophisticated parts (actuators, sensors, etc.) into a robust and reproducible system.

2️⃣ Scale needed for economic viability: it is necessary to link large-scale production (more than 1 million units/year) with cost reduction to make Optimus affordable, with a target price of about 20,000 dollars. Economies of scale are crucial for humanoid robots to be affordable, and reaching these volumes is a monumental challenge due to the production infrastructure and supply chain required. This emphasis underlines that without mass production, Optimus will not achieve the economic impact Tesla is seeking.

3️⃣ Pressure to meet ambitious expectations: Musk has set aggressive timelines, such as limited internal production in 2025 and high production for other companies in 2026. His emphasis on the difficulty serves to temper public and investor expectations, acknowledging that, although Optimus prototypes are already performing tasks in factories, scaling to millions of units requires overcoming significant obstacles. This is a response to the public perception that progress is slow, clarifying that production at scale is a critical bottleneck.

4️⃣ Comparison with other Tesla projects: Musk frequently compares the difficulty of production at scale with other Tesla challenges, such as electric vehicles. "Production at scale is 100 times harder than making a prototype", a recurring theme in his narrative about cars, rockets and now robots. His experience with Model 3 production ("production hell") leads him to emphasize that humanoid robots, with their greater complexity, face even bigger challenges.

5️⃣ Competition and market narrative: the race for humanoid robots is growing, with competitors such as Figure, Boston Dynamics and Chinese companies advancing. On X, users have pointed out other manufacturers' progress, which may be leading Musk to emphasize the difficulties in order to highlight Tesla's advantage in tackling complex problems. By highlighting the challenges, he reinforces the narrative that Tesla is attacking a unique and transformative problem, justifying any delay as part of the process.

6️⃣ Long-term strategic vision: Musk sees Optimus as a product that will change the world more than Tesla's cars. His focus on the difficulty of production at scale reflects his vision of a future in which humanoid robots are ubiquitous, requiring massive volumes to impact industries such as manufacturing, logistics and domestic services. By emphasizing this, he sets the stage to justify massive investments and patience from stakeholders.

How do you feel after reading this analysis?

First of all, we have to admit that Grok's ability to analyze four tweets shows quite a lot of imagination, but beyond that, what I wonder is to what extent Elon is being frank in his words or simply creating a smokescreen to take advantage of his media position. I am referring to what he already tried to do in 2023 with the pause on AI development, shortly after the launch of ChatGPT, which turned out to be a ploy to try to regain the advantage he had lost by leaving OpenAI.

But anyway, let's assume what he says is true and try to propose solutions regarding the scaling of robot manufacturing.

💰 First of all, if we are talking about the need for infrastructure to grow robot manufacturing, then the first thing needed is money. And money, real money, does not seem to be lacking. It is true that it is not arriving by the ton, as is happening in the AI sector, but not a week goes by without us learning of big investment rounds in humanoid robot startups. The one for Dyna Robotics, of 120 million dollars from NVIDIA, Amazon, Salesforce, Samsung, LG, Robostrategy, CRV and First Round Capital, is one of the most recent.

🤖 Second, but even more important than money, is the use of AI, not only because it is the brain of the robots, but because it is being used by the engineers who design robots to accelerate their manufacture. This matter is very relevant, since what we are seeing is how AI accelerates everything, and the higher the technical level of the person using it, the better the results; therefore, now, thanks to engineers using AI, we are much closer to being able to scale robot manufacturing massively.

🏭 The need for technology suppliers right now can probably be seen more as a bottleneck than as a positive factor. I think many manufacturers will not be able to manufacture all the components of the robot, something Tesla, which specializes in vertical integration in its production, probably can do. Therefore most manufacturers will depend heavily on suppliers of GPUs, sensors, actuators, batteries, … for their robots to be feasible in the short term.

💹 Market interest will ultimately be what determines the pace of manufacturing, since I very much doubt that these companies, however much funding they have, will set out to mass-produce robots if they do not have orders from large industrial companies wanting to integrate this technology into their value chain. Then the interest of individuals also remains to be confirmed, something where we are starting to see the first signs thanks to the work Unitree is doing as a pioneer in selling robots openly.

So as you can see, there is still a long way to go in the manufacture of humanoid robots; the good thing is that the steps being taken seem to be the right ones, so it is only a matter of time before we reach a happy ending.
