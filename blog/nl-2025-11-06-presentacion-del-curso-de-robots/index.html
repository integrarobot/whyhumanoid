Contents of the Humanoid Robots course

INTRODUCTION

Why now?

Explores the reasons why the development of humanoid robots is accelerating precisely at this moment: advances in AI, cost reductions, technological maturity and an urgent need for labour in multiple sectors.

Why humanoid?

Explores why the human body has been the model chosen for many robots. From its ability to adapt to environments designed for humans to more intuitive social interaction, the evolutionary, ergonomic and cultural reasons behind humanoid design are explained.

State of the art

An updated overview of the current capabilities of humanoid robots. What can they do today? What limitations do they have? Examples of the most advanced models in operation are presented.

MODULE 1

AI hardware

Physical devices that make it possible to run artificial intelligence models in real time. They include specialized chips such as GPUs, TPUs or NPUs, designed to process large volumes of data with low energy consumption.

AI software

The set of algorithms, models and systems that allow the robot to learn, make decisions, adapt to new situations and respond to its environment. It may include vision, language, planning and control models.

Vision system

Cameras and sensors that allow the robot to perceive its environment visually. They are combined with computer vision software to identify objects, people, obstacles and movements.

Language system

Technologies that allow the robot to understand and generate natural language. They include language processing models such as LLMs (Large Language Models) and speech synthesis and recognition systems.

Other sensors

In addition to vision, robots use pressure, temperature, proximity, force, orientation and other sensors to interact safely and precisely with the physical world.

Arms

Limbs designed to replicate the human function of manipulation. They usually include multiple joints and degrees of freedom that allow a great variety of movements.

Hands

Systems for grasping and fine manipulation. Some robotic hands have haptic sensors, controlled force and the ability to perform delicate or complex tasks.

Motors

Actuators that enable the robot's movement. They can be electric, hydraulic or pneumatic, and are optimized to deliver force, speed and precision according to the part of the body.

Legs

Elements that allow the robot to walk or stand. Their design involves solving problems of balance, shock absorption and locomotion on different types of terrain.

Cooling

Systems that dissipate the heat generated by the motors and computing chips. It is key to maintaining efficiency and extending the robot's useful life, especially in intensive tasks.

Feet

The support base that gives the robot stability. In bipedal models, they are designed to imitate human gait and to adjust dynamically to the terrain to avoid falls.

Connectivity

Antennas or modules for Wi-Fi / 5G / Bluetooth, important for operating on a network or receiving commands.

MODULE 2

Physical AI

A concept that joins artificial intelligence with robotic bodies that act in the real world. Unlike purely digital AI, physical AI must face the uncertainty of the physical environment and learn from it autonomously.

Who develops them?

A review of the main companies, startups, research centres and universities leading the creation of humanoid robots, from mechanical designs to locomotion and perception systems.

Who programs them?

A deeper look at the technical profiles involved in developing robot software: software engineers, AI specialists, robotics experts and embedded systems developers.

Training

An explanation of how humanoid robots learn to move, manipulate objects, communicate and collaborate. Methods such as reinforcement learning, sim2real learning or training in simulated environments are covered. Digital twins and world models.

Demonstrations

An analysis of the role of public demonstrations in the advancement and validation of this technology. Key events such as CES and presentations by Tesla, Figure, Boston Dynamics, among others, are reviewed.

Simulators

Whether because it is not yet possible to buy a humanoid robot or because tests are needed to validate how it works, there are a number of simulators that help us learn to control robots, even if only virtually.

Swarm intelligence

The technique that enables decentralized coordination among several humanoid robots working as a group to solve tasks efficiently, without the need for central control.

Current challenges

The main technical, economic and social challenges facing this technology. From energy autonomy and robust locomotion to public acceptance and ethical dilemmas. Movarec's paradox and the uncanny valley.

MODULE 3

Usefulness offered

A summary of the concrete benefits humanoid robots can bring: automation of physical tasks, support in the home, improved productivity, assistance for older people or people with reduced mobility, among others.

When will they arrive?

The expected adoption timeline is analyzed, from the first industrial and logistics pilots to arrival in the domestic environment, with realistic estimates of large-scale deployment.

How to invest?

Options for participating financially in the sector: from investments in emerging and technology companies to thematic funds and public or private opportunities.

Competition between countries

A geopolitical analysis of the global race to lead this technology. Which countries are most advanced, how they are organizing to gain competitive advantage and what role regulation and technological sovereignty play.

Market volume

Estimates of market size in the short, medium and long term. Includes projections on revenue, demand by sector, annual growth and main segments.

Funding

The ways in which projects and companies are obtaining financial resources: venture capital, government funds, accelerators, big tech companies and international initiatives.

Business model

How money is expected to be made with humanoid robots: direct sales, robots as a service (RaaS), subscriptions, maintenance, customization and new hybrid models.
