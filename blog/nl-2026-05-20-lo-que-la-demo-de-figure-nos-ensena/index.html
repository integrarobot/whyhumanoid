What Figure's demo teaches us about humanoid robots

193,000 packages processed in 155 hours of work. That is where the counter stands right now on the longest robotics demo in history.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 20 May 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

This article was going to be titled "what Figure's 8-hour demo teaches us about robots", but I soon had to change it because the 8 hours became 24 and then an uninterrupted broadcast in which the robots are demonstrating their skill in the art of handling postal packages.

This kind of robotic Truman Show has a lot to teach us, and who knows, perhaps it will end up becoming that ChatGPT moment of robotics that has been talked about for a little over a year.

But beyond the fact itself of having some robots there working night and day, I think it is worth stopping for a while to understand what this means and how it could become an important milestone for the development of humanoid robots.

  1. 8 hours was the initial time planned for the robot demo, because it puts it on a par with a working day, which makes it possible to extract productivity metrics, but it is also related to the need for supervision. If the robot is there working, there are many other things people will have to do for the operation to work, at least for the moment.
  2. Comparing it directly with human productivity was not initially planned, but as Brett Adcock, Figure's founder, got carried away with the success the broadcast was having, he decided to do the exercise of putting a human worker to compete with the robots. In this first duel the human, named Aime, won, but I dare say there will soon be a rematch, and given how fast robots learn, I think the scoreboard will turn in their favour.
  3. Although the robots have been working there autonomously for more than 100 hours, there are people who don't trust it, and that's fine because it allows faults to be found, but sometimes they also get it wrong. For example, in this case they were assuming the robot was teleoperated because of a movement it made with its hand to protect its head when it came too close to the metal structure of the conveyor belt.
  4. Watch the video: at certain moments the company's workers are gathered back there, with a few robots around them, chatting in the most relaxed way. I'd love to know what they are talking about, but beyond that, I wonder whether these facilities where the conveyor belt is belong to Figure or to a customer. What is clear is that the package sorting machine is real and the work the robot does is important, because currently it means there have to be people doing that very tedious job.
  5. But beyond all these anecdotal aspects, what we should really take away is that we are witnessing live how humanoid robotics is trying to cross the chasm that until now has separated demos from operation in the real world. And that is what we are all waiting for: real cases where the quality of the technology can be evaluated. I don't know about you, but for me this clears up a lot of doubts.
  6. In any case, it is best not to confuse this spectacular broadcast with a closed industrial integration. We still lack data, such as the exact error rate, environmental conditions, the number of human interventions, maintenance between shifts, total cost, safety, integration with the customer's systems and behaviour in rare cases.

Now for the final conclusion. I sense that demand for humanoid robots is going to far exceed supply. Here we have Figure showing off its capabilities, and a few days after learning the news that UPS wants to buy its robots by the thousands. But production capacity is still very low: although it has gone from 1 robot/day to 1 robot/hour, there is still a long way to go to respond to the millions of companies and homes that are going to want this augmented productivity machine.

And what about the supply chain? The tensions we have experienced in recent years in the electronics sector will seem insignificant when everyone wants their own humanoid robot to take the dirty work off their hands.
