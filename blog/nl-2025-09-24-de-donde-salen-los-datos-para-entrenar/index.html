Where the data to train Humanoid Robots comes from

Today I'm giving you an idea for getting started in the Physical AI business: capturing data on human activity for robot training.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 24 September 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

When we think about AI training, we might make the mistake of believing it is a single process, very slow and very expensive, but linear and controlled by a single company. However, it is more complicated than it seems, since several entities have to collaborate for the LLM to work. For example, you need companies that provide training data, as in the case of Scale AI (recently bought by Meta), or companies that provide staff to carry out the part of training called RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback)

Something similar is going to happen with the training of humanoid robots, since it is becoming clear that without real-world data it will be impossible for these machines to properly fulfil the function expected of them.

Let's see an example of this with the HDMI research led by Haoyang Weng, which explains it as follows: enabling robust interaction between humanoids and objects remains a challenge due to the scarcity of motion data and the highly contact-sensitive nature of the task. HDMI (HumanoiD iMitation for Interaction) is a simple and general framework that learns whole-body humanoid-object interaction skills directly from monocular RGB videos.

So where is the opportunity? We have robots and we have the software to train them, but the data is missing, in this case videos of human operations. In fact, what I find when I talk to companies interested in implementing this technology in their facilities is that they describe very varied situations, which is why I think that, in order to integrate humanoid robots into their activity, they will need recordings of how the productive activity is carried out. And this is where I see the opportunity: companies devoted to recording those very specific videos to train robots.

The case of Figure and Brookfield Asset Management

When last week the 1 billion dollar MEGAROUND in humanoid robot manufacturer Figure was announced, I noticed that among the investors who had put in the most money, Brookfield Asset Management stood out.

In case you don't know, Brookfield is an investment company focused on property, renewable energy, infrastructure and venture capital, so it made sense for it to have invested in Figure if we see it as a purely financial investment, or even because of its connection with data centre infrastructure.

But a few days after the news of the investment round, it was announced that Figure's collaboration with Brookfield goes much further, because it involves working together on one of the most important open fronts for humanoid robotics to become a reality.

The initiative is called Go-Big and focuses on large-scale humanoid training, for which the aim is to create the world's largest and most diverse humanoid pre-training dataset, driven by a collaboration with Brookfield, which owns more than 100,000 homes.

Basically, it is about using the facilities Brookfield has around the world to record videos of people doing things, so that these videos can then be used by Figure to train its artificial intelligence called Helix.

Now the question we have to ask ourselves is: where are the other companies creating world models for robot training going to get their data from?

And one answer may come from another current news item: the presentation of the new version of Meta's augmented reality glasses with Ray-ban and Oakley, as well as future ones coming from Apple and other companies.

I admit I don't like wearables; I don't even have a smartwatch, so I'm even less likely to wear those glasses all day, but I see great usefulness in this technology for robot training. I'm thinking of a company that could start collecting data now on how its operators work, so that later it can use that data to train robots when they are more developed.

It is like what happens with context engineering in LLMs. It is one thing for the machine to be intelligent and another for it to know what we are talking about. So if giving the LLM good context is essential for it to understand the task it has to do, imagine how decisive it must be for a robot, which is a much more complex system.
