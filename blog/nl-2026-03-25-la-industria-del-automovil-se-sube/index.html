The car industry rides the Physical AI wave

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 25 March 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

This afternoon at 19:00 I am going to hold a webinar on Youtube where I will explain how you can ride the Physical AI wave. Don't miss it!

Why Physical AI?

NVIDIA is especially interested in promoting this term rather than robotics. There is no doubt that what is fashionable now is AI, and it is also what has made the company the most valuable in the world, so Physical AI is presented as a continuation. We are no longer talking about programmable robots but intelligent robots, so things change radically, because we go from limited machines to general-purpose robots, which scale much better.

The car industry rides the wave

The automotive sector is facing yet another transformation, but unlike what happened with combustion systems, in this case they seem to have learned the lesson and are not taking so long to start competing with Tesla.

It is true that none of the Western manufacturers has yet made a decision as radical as Tesla's in prioritizing robots over cars, but quite a few companies are already taking steps in robotics, even though it may still seem early to start investing in it.

Now, what signs do we have for saying that the car industry is so interested in humanoid robots? Let's see:

  1. BMW has been taking steps in robotics for several years, for example with its stake in the mobile robot company Idealworks, which later came to be owned by Agile Robots, and the collaboration between these companies. The car manufacturer is also working on pilots deploying humanoid robots from Figue and Hexagon, showing a clear interest in being an active part of this robotics sector.
  2. Mercedes has been working for some time with Apptronik to integrate Apollo humanoid robots into its production plants to perform repetitive and logistics tasks, such as transporting components and using tools. It is also one of the company's main partners, having taken part in the investment rounds that valued it at 5.5 billion, in which John Deere also took part.
  3. Renault has invested in the French startup Wandercraft and has also worked jointly with it on the development of the Calvin-40 robot, which can be trained to pick mixed parts from containers, but for now it is limited to lifting tyres and carrying panels in the body shop, since its limiting factors are speed and dexterity.
  4. Rivian, when it comes to robots, has not settled for doing what other companies do, investing in startups or collaborating with them; instead it has created a spin-off called Mind Robotics, which in a few months has raised more than 600 million dollars, with the aim of tackling a structural deficiency of current industrial automation solutions, that is, providing what classical robotics cannot do: human-like dexterity, adaptation and physical reasoning.
  5. Volkswagen works with humanoid robots through a Joint Venture it has with the Chinese manufacturer FAW, and it is through this collaboration that they work on the development of UBTECH humanoids, using the Walker S robot to perform tasks such as component assembly and handling automotive parts.
  6. Hyundai was one of the first car manufacturers to bet on humanoid robots when in 2021 it bought humanoid pioneer Boston Dynamics from Softbank. Since then it has not stopped advancing in robotics, because it also collaborates with other companies such as Persona AI on the development of welding robots.
  7. Toyota works actively in humanoid robotics through its R&D centre, Toyota Research Institute, where it has an alliance with Boston Dynamics to accelerate the development of general-purpose humanoids, combining TRI's Large Behavior Models with the Atlas robot. The company also collaborates with Agility to use its robots in its car factories in Canada.
  8. Xpeng is also very interested in the development of physical AI, as shown by the development of its own humanoid robot Iron, designed for industrial and service tasks, which stands out for its flexible skin, 82 degrees of freedom, bionic hands and solid-state battery. Its mass production is scheduled to begin during this year.
  9. Ford has collaborated for years with Agility on the development of the Digit robot, in last-mile logistics solutions combined with autonomous vehicle technology.

As you can see, a large part of the car industry is focused on this new physical AI technology, and the goal is clear: to deploy humanoid robots in factories to take care of routine tasks that are dangerous for people but which large robotic arms cannot tackle due to their lack of versatility.

But the most interesting thing about this is that the automotive industry is only the spearhead for other sectors to gradually ride this wave, for example logistics, as we will see later with Amazon and Zalando.
