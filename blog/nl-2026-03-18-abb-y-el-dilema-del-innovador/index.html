ABB and the innovator's dilemma

Welcome to the era of physical AI, NVIDIA's new slogan, which traditional robotics companies must also gradually take on board.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 18 March 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

When a company like Amazon decides to spend all its money (200 billion dollars in 2026) to create AWS version 2.0, it is making clear to us that it is not falling victim to the innovator's dilemma. The company that practically invented Cloud Computing is going all in because it is convinced that with AI that infrastructure needs to be reinvented, whatever it costs.

However, we are not seeing something similar in the robotics industry; although signs of some interest in evolving toward Physical AI are beginning to appear, there is still little news of companies in the sector betting decisively on this technology.

With the news of the collaboration between ABB Robotics and NVIDIA we are starting to see some change, but it still seems insufficient to me, and we could not yet say that this company is not falling prey to the innovator's dilemma.

Its business of selling programmable industrial robots works; what reason would it have to take risks by investing in a new type of AI-driven general-purpose robot?

For decades, ABB has been synonymous with industrial robotics: precision arm, welding cell, assembly line. It has spent fifty years manufacturing the machines that move the world's factories, but that type of robotics is not capable of learning and therefore of improving by itself.

The collaboration between ABB and NVIDIA, in which the integration of the NVIDIA Omniverse libraries into the ABB RobotStudio platform has just been announced, should be a first step in that evolution from programmable robotics to Physical AI.

This collaboration seeks to solve the sim-to-real problem: the gap between what a robot learns in simulation and what it is able to execute in the physical world. A gap that, for years, has been the main bottleneck for deploying AI-based robotics at industrial scale.

What ABB is doing is not just a software update. It is the search for a new positioning, because traditional robot manufacturers know the game has changed, and that they are staking everything on Physical AI, that is, artificial intelligence models that not only process data but act on the world with precision and generalization.

This transition has direct implications for the future of humanoid robots. Because the problems ABB is solving today in industrial arms, such as synthetic data generation, training in digital twins and reliable transfer to real hardware, are exactly the same problems humanoids need to solve to leave the laboratory and enter factories, performing tasks with precision and versatility.

NVIDIA does not suffer from the innovator's dilemma

Image: Image

However many years NVIDIA has now been ending its events by talking about robotics, it is still exciting to see everything the world's most valuable company is contributing to this sector. And as can be seen in the image, humanoid robots occupy the central space because they are the priority for the future due to the business volume they may represent for the company.

It is worth mentioning here that in the GTC 2026 presentation, in addition to ABB, robotic arms from FANUC and KUKA also appeared, so it is clear that the bulk of the traditional industrial robotics industry is also working on Physical AI, taking advantage of NVIDIA's capabilities.

And everything NVIDIA is doing in robotics can be summarized in the three layers Jensen Huang explained:

  1. Training software
  2. Synthetic data generation and simulation
  3. The robotic computer that goes inside the robot itself: Jetson Thor

For each of these levels the company has developed its own technology: software for the first two and hardware for the third.

As for software, the most important novelty was the presentation of NVIDIA Newton, the simulator that adds highly interactive manipulation and locomotion capabilities for industrial robotics, and which fits into the complete stack the company offers as follows:

  • Isaac Lab for robot training and evaluation.
  • Newton for GPU-accelerated differentiable physics simulation.
  • Cosmos, a world model for neural simulation.
  • Groot, robotics foundation models for reasoning and action.

The final video shown at the event is quite a declaration of intent about how clear the company is that its future lies in Physical AI. I don't think it is simply because the robots look cute singing and playing instruments as if they were cartoons.

Welcome to the era of physical AI is the representation of how NVIDIA perceives the future. It's true that we don't yet see it day to day in our streets, but if they don't make it possible, who will?
