A method for integrating robots

My proposal for starting down the road of deploying robots in companies.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 1 April 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

When I launched the Integrarobot project, I chose the name thinking about what the process of integrating robots into companies, homes and, in general, the real economy would be like.

But the symbol accompanying the robot in the logo has a deeper meaning.

I chose the integral symbol not for purely aesthetic reasons, but because I sensed that robotics would not arrive as an instant revolution, but as a progressive evolution.

In mathematics, an integral represents the continuous accumulation of small contributions over time. It does not measure an instant. It measures everything that adds up.

And when I look at how robotics is developing, it is easy to see what is happening. Companies don't go from zero robots to a fully automated factory.

  • First, a robot appears that solves a specific task.
  • Then another that automates part of the process.
  • Later, the workflow is reorganized.
  • And little by little the impact starts to accumulate.

That is why robotics adoption works like an integral.

  • Every small improvement adds up.
  • Every automated task adds more value.
  • Every operational lesson accumulates over time.

And over time that sum ends up transforming the company.

We have learned of many cases in recent months of humanoid robots being integrated into industry; for example, we have talked about the work pilots of Figure's robots in BMW's factories or Agility's in Toyota's.

I also find the initiatives being carried out by the English startup Humanoid very interesting, because it is the one communicating most about them. Thus we see that they have already run work pilots of their humanoid robot at the plants of Schaeffler, Siemens and Ford, and the most recent is the work they have done with SAP and Martur Fompak, where they completed a proof of concept in a real production logistics environment.

If we multiply these proofs of concept by all the companies currently manufacturing humanoid robots, we will probably have many hundreds of cases in which this technology is being tested, which becomes a solid working base that will later allow it to be transferred to all kinds of companies and environments.

Following this example, when an SME, or even a family, considers buying a robot, there is a lot of work to do first. In fact, there is a fairly clear method to apply to get started.

1️⃣ First you have to generate context. Because, just as with AI, the quality of the robot's work will depend on how clear the task to be performed is and how specific the limits of what it has to do are. If the robot doesn't have the details of how an operation is done, it will have to improvise, and I think robot hallucinations can have much worse consequences than AI ones. At this point I recommend using AI to document the company's entire operational process, which will also help detect the bottlenecks where robots can add the most value at the start.

2️⃣ Next, it is necessary to document the real work on video. Once we are clear about all the processes that take place in the company and have identified where robots could take part to do things more efficiently, it is time to record the operation on video, because that will be the knowledge that will have to be transmitted to the robot first-hand for its training. At this point a situation of conflict arises over how workers may perceive the arrival of robots. Since we still have little experience here, I'm afraid we will have to learn about it as we go.

3️⃣ And finally, it is essential to simulate. Before buying a robot, it makes sense to model how it can fit into the company's current work system. And here another of the utilities AI can offer you is creating simulators of how robots work. Although you can turn to one of the simulators robotics companies have created, what I recommend is that you build it yourself, for example as a Claude artifact. To do this you will have to give it the context from point 1 and ask it to program it as if the work were going to be done with a robotic arm. This way you will better understand how the robot will work before you take the plunge and buy it, and this will save you future problems you may not have taken into account.

So integrating robots does not begin when the robot arrives, but much earlier, when the company begins to understand its processes and to improve small parts of the system.

Because, as in an integral, the real impact does not appear at a single point, but when all the improvements accumulate.
