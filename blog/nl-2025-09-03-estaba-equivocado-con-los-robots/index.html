I was wrong about Chinese robots

Seeing what many robotics scientists and engineers are doing with Unitree's G1 robot, I am changing my mind about the future of Chinese robots.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 3 September 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

If there is one thing China's robot manufacturers are demonstrating, it is courage. While in the West companies devote themselves to making demos that seem rather staged and where we don't know whether the robots are teleoperated, in China they have dared to hold an Olympic games to show off all the good (and the bad) of their robots.

In this way my perception of how the humanoid robotics business is going to take shape has been changing: I no longer think Chinese robots are going to be second-rate; rather, I think some of them really can be at the level Western companies are able to reach.

The main reason for this change of opinion is that I am convinced that experimentation in the real world is the best way to arrive at the solutions best suited to people's needs. Laboratories are all very well, but when we are talking about solving problems for millions of people, the sooner we face the difficulties of the real world the better.

And this is what is happening with the company Unitree, which has become a walking laboratory, since its robots are starting to reach all kinds of places, where robotics engineers can begin to experiment with them and put them in all kinds of situations, in search of solutions and uses they can offer people.

One of hundreds of examples of this situation is the work done by the scientist Zhi Su to teach the Unitree G1 robot to play Table Tennis, a skill that goes far beyond entertainment, since the manual dexterity involved in that sport can be extrapolated to all kinds of activities in the home, industry, services, …

Image: Image

Another example is the work of the startup FieldAI, which has raised 405 million dollars from investors including Jeff Bezos to develop artificial intelligence models that can be used in general-purpose robots. The company's mission, like that of others such as Skild AI, is to build a single robotic brain that can generalize to different types of robots and to a diversity of environments.

Along the same lines we find the company OpenMind, whose goal is to create the Android of robotics, which they have called OM1 and which is designed to facilitate the integration of intelligent machines into everyday life. The company recently closed a 20 million dollar investment round, and Unitree's G1 robot also appears in its announcements; it is becoming the de facto standard in this sector.

Tesla's new Master Plan has Optimus among its fundamental pillars

Image: Image

Optimus is now part of the new Master Plan published by Tesla in its version IV.

Autonomy must benefit all of humanity. The tools we make at Tesla help us create the products that drive human prosperity. The way we develop and use autonomy, and the new capabilities it makes available to us, must be grounded in its ability to improve the human condition. Improving everyday life and making it safer for all people through our autonomous technology has always been, and remains, our goal. Greater access drives greater growth. Creating technologically advanced, affordable products available at scale is necessary to build a prosperous and unconstrained society. It serves to further democratize society while improving everyone's quality of life.

Thus the pillars on which Tesla's work will focus from now on are:

🚘 Electric vehicles

🔋 Energy products

🦾 Humanoid robots.

And this is where Optimus comes in, the autonomous humanoid robot that is changing not only the perception of work itself, but also its availability and capacity. Particularly monotonous or dangerous jobs can now be done by other means. In this way, Optimus's mission is to give people back more time to do what they love.

NVIDIA presents its GPU specially designed for humanoid robots

Image: Image

A radical change for humanoid robotics is how Apptronik has described the presentation of the NVIDIA Jetson Thor AGX, a GPU designed to serve as the brain of humanoid robots.

Jensen Huang himself starred in the campaign in which the company sent this machine as a gift to a large number of humanoid robot manufacturers.

We are talking about a computer for robots powered by an NVIDIA Blackwell GPU that delivers 2,070 FP4 teraflops to handle complex applications including agentic AI, high-speed sensor processing and general robotic tasks.

Its launch price is $3,499, which gives us an idea of the order of magnitude the complete robot may reach, knowing that this is the price of its "brain". I dare say that with this move NVIDIA aspires to capture around 10% of the business volume generated in the Physical AI and robotics sector.
