Data to train robots: the resurgence of data science

The protagonist of robotics in 2026 is neither hardware nor software; it is training data.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 10 June 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

With the arrival of LLMs it seemed that data science had taken a back seat, since the power of these new models is achieved in the labs of the companies that create them, not so much with the subsequent training the organizations using them might give them. In an earlier era, it was indeed essential to be able to train models with your own data, but now fine-tuning seems restricted to the wealthiest AI labs.

However, with the emergence of Physical AI we find ourselves in a situation similar to the one prior to LLMs as far as robot training is concerned, since if we want to use them we will have no choice but to train them with our own operational data. Who knows, perhaps soon we will have access to generalist models that already come with all the necessary knowledge, but for now we will have to put in the hard graft and build our own first-person video database to train robots.

At this point it is worth looking at the strategy NVIDIA shared at the GTC Taipei event, where they explained that to advance in robotics it is no longer enough to have better models; you have to build the entire cycle needed for robots, autonomous vehicles and industrial systems to be able to learn, simulate, be validated and be deployed in the real world.

In this regard, the most relevant things presented at the event on Physical AI were:

  1. Cosmos 3: open models capable of working with text, image, video, sound and action. Their value lies not only in generating video, but in helping to reason about future states, simulate worlds and train physical systems with synthetic data.
  2. Alpamayo: a strong bet on closed-loop training for autonomous vehicles. The main idea is that the model must learn from the consequences of its own decisions in simulation, not just imitate recorded data.
  3. JetPack 7.2: more tools for deploying agentic AI at the edge: robots, cameras, industrial machines and autonomous devices. Here the critical issue is moving from the lab to real hardware.
  4. Agent Skills: NVIDIA is turning complex simulation, data generation, training, validation and deployment workflows into tasks that AI agents can execute and developers can review.
  5. ovrtx: an SDK for integrating RTX sensor simulation (cameras, lidar, radar) into your own applications, using OpenUSD and workflows compatible with simulation, CAD and visualization.
  6. Isaac GR00T Reference Humanoid Robot: which we talked about last week, an integrated platform for humanoid research combining hardware, hands, computing and software. The aim is for robotics engineers to spend less time assembling basic parts and more time researching policies and behaviour.

In this way, what we are seeing day by day is that physical AI advances not only thanks to the development of larger models, but as a complete infrastructure is developed for generating data, simulating rare scenarios, training in closed loop, validating behaviours and deploying on real machines.

The challenge of collecting data for robot training

Image: Image

At this point we must insist again on the importance of collecting data to train robots, and here you need to know that what happened with LLMs cannot be extrapolated to VLAs or WAMs, since currently the data does not exist, something that was the case with the digital data in text and image format used to train ChatGPT and company.

So, to train Physical AI models, it will be necessary to generate data from scratch, unless we are able to find a way for YouTube or TikTok videos of people doing things to be used for this as well.

And the current ways in which the industry works to capture data for training robots are:

  1. Teleoperation: a person controls the robot remotely and it records all the movements made. It is the most direct way to teach skills, but also the most expensive, because every hour of data requires an hour of human work. Examples: AgiBot World, DROID.
  1. Manual simulation: instead of controlling the robot directly, a person's movements are recorded using simpler, cheaper devices that simulate the robot's hands. This greatly reduces the cost of data collection because you don't have to work directly with the robot, although human movements must then be translated to the robot's joints. Examples: Mobile ALOHA, UMI.
  1. Data from deployed robots: when robots start working in factories, warehouses or real environments, they generate data continuously. Every error, every exception and every task performed becomes training material. Tesla hopes to take advantage of millions of hours of experience accumulated by its autonomous vehicles and future Optimus robots. Figure is also following this strategy.
  1. Simulation. Robots learn inside virtual environments before facing the real world. The advantage is great, since millions of hours of experience can be generated at very low cost. However, the problem is that simulated physics still does not reproduce reality perfectly. Aspects such as friction, slipping or complex contacts remain hard to model. Examples: NVIDIA Isaac Sim, Genesis.
  1. First-person video: consists of recording what a person sees while working. It is much easier to obtain thousands of hours of this type of video than to teleoperate robots. The drawback is that they show what happens, but do not directly contain the robotic actions that would need to be executed. Examples: Ego4D, Project Aria.

At this point it is worth dwelling on the work of the startup Gilabs, which has put on sale the EGO1 device, presented as the first egocentric capture helmet for Physical AI, a device that effortlessly transforms first-person activities, especially manipulations, into training data for foundation models.

  1. Internet video: YouTube, TikTok and other platforms contain an immense amount of information about how humans interact with the world. It is the cheapest and most scalable source, since it allows very diverse objects, tools, tasks and situations to be observed. However, it does not usually include information on forces, exact trajectories or joint movements needed to control a robot.
  1. Synthetic data generated by world models: Here one of the most promising lines appears. Instead of merely simulating graphics, world models try to predict how a situation will physically evolve when the robot acts. If these systems achieve sufficient precision, they could generate enormous amounts of synthetic experience without the need to collect so much real data. It is an idea very much aligned with the evolution from VLAs to the World Models we are already seeing in robotics. Example: NVIDIA Cosmos.

Current state of development of world models

"*Language gave machines a way to talk about that world. World models are how machines will finally come to understand it, imagine it, reason with it and interact with it*".

Speaking of world models for robot training, it turns out the great Fei-Fei Li has just given us a new article explaining how the construction of world models is evolving, so let's comment on it here because it is most interesting.

What the doctor explains to us is that the term "world model" is being used for too many different things. And this is starting to be a problem, because a model that generates spectacular videos is not the same as a system capable of simulating the physics of a scene, or a model that helps a robot decide what action it should execute.

Put simply: not everything that seems to understand the world really understands it.

For example, a model can generate a beautiful city seen from a drone, with buildings, streets, trees and cars, but if we try to walk through that same city from the ground, everything may start to break. The facades don't fit, the streets have no continuity, the proportions fail or the objects don't have coherent geometry.

That may be enough to make a video, but not to train a robot.

A robot doesn't just need a convincing image of the world. It needs a useful representation of the world. It needs to know where objects are, what shape they have, how much space they take up, how they move, what happens if it pushes them, whether they are heavy, whether they slip, whether they break, whether they block the way or whether they can be grasped.

That is why Fei-Fei Li and her team at World Labs propose distinguishing three different functions within world models: rendering, simulating and planning.

  • The renderer generates what we see.
  • The simulator represents how the world works.
  • The planner decides what action an agent should execute within that world.

And this distinction is fundamental for understanding where Physical AI is heading. So we must keep a very close eye on the work of World Labs and the other companies working on developing WAMs, because the future of robotics depends on them.
