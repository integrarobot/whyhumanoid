We need Intelligent Agents to have Humanoid Robots

In 2024 there was so much talk about agents that now it is time to make them a reality, and not only that: they will also be fundamental to the development of robotics.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 19 February 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

Jensen Huang explains at CES 2025 how physical AI and Intelligent Agents are driven by NVIDIA computing.

Just as the arrival of LLMs has sent the development of humanoid robots soaring, since they now have a superior intelligence that allows them to understand their environment and the instructions given to them in natural language, there is still a step further to go for these machines to be fully functional; and for that we need intelligent agents, which are the evolution of intelligent assistants.

The key word for moving from assistants to agents is autonomy. When we remove the "co" from "copilot" and leave only the pilot in charge of the vehicle. Or, put another way, when we put it on autopilot.

I like this copilot analogy because it represents the current situation very well, where intelligent assistants do not take control of the vehicle but assist the pilot so that the corresponding task is carried out more efficiently. The intelligent assistants we now use daily, such as ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, … are gradually going to take the initiative, for example when they have a search function, because they not only answer questions or requests based on their knowledge, but have to go out and look for it on the Internet.

But that was a first stage, and now we are living through the second phase, which began last year with Anthropic's presentation of the Computer Use model for Claude 3.5 Sonnet, which is able to interact with tools and can manipulate a computer desktop environment. The downside is that the results obtained so far with this feature have not been very good, and it seems the company has stopped its rollout.

On the other hand, we find OpenAI's Operator model, which does seem to have produced better results by focusing on interaction directly on the Internet, browsing and interacting with different web pages, always with user supervision, but without taking control of the software installed on our computer. Autonomy is therefore relative, because the user has to authorize the actions, but it seems clear that this is the path toward having autonomous intelligent agents capable of making decisions and acting accordingly.

Acting is precisely what we expect from a Humanoid Robot: that it does not merely follow the instructions provided in its programming or training, but is able to take the initiative when necessary. For example, if it finds an object in its path, it will have to decide whether to move it aside, avoid it or pick it up. Whether at home, in a shop or in a factory, we will want it to act like a (non-lazy) human, so that if the object has fallen, the normal thing will be to pick it up, not avoid it or move it aside.

That is why we can say that the development of intelligent agents will drive humanoid robotics and that the robot's brain will be an agent.

To see it with an example, since OpenAI's Operator is only available in the most expensive paid version of ChatGPT, let's look at what can be done with Perplexity's Deep Research mode, which is available in the free version of this intelligent assistant.

If you look at the image, what you can see is that the agent proposes a series of tasks, which it then validates, and finally generates a corresponding piece of work in order to deliver the result the user asked for.

Components that make up an intelligent agent

We have seen that an intelligent agent is key for humanoid robots to be truly autonomous, but what makes it possible? To understand it, let's look at its fundamental components:

👁️ Perception: this is the agent's ability to gather information from its environment through sensors or external data sources. In a humanoid robot, this may include computer vision, speech recognition, proximity sensors and object detection. Without perception, the agent would operate blind, without knowing what is happening around it. In the case of an intelligent agent that runs on a computer or a mobile phone, what is usually done is to observe web browsing, the equivalent of taking a screenshot and asking it to interpret what is seen in it.

🧠 Processing: once it has gathered data, the agent needs to analyze it and make sense of it. This is where advanced deep learning models come into play, interpreting the information, identifying patterns and understanding the context it finds itself in. This processing is what allows the agent to understand the context related to the action being requested of it, and on that basis to subsequently make a sound decision about it.

🔀 Decision-making: understanding the environment is not enough; one must also act accordingly. An intelligent agent chooses the best action according to its goals, its limitations and what it has previously learned. This is where autonomy begins to make sense: it is not just about executing predefined orders, but about evaluating each situation and deciding the best course of action without depending on human intervention at every moment.

🦾 Actuation: finally, the agent translates its decision into a physical or digital action. In a humanoid robot, this means moving, manipulating objects or communicating with other systems. In a digital agent, it can mean interacting with applications, performing searches or automating processes. Without the ability to act, the agent would remain just a passive assistant, not a truly autonomous system.

Although these four elements are essential, the real evolutionary leap in intelligent agents will come when they can integrate continuous learning and long-term memory. Today, most assistants and agents only remember information within a usage session or within very limited ranges. For a humanoid robot to operate fully autonomously, it will need to develop a memory of experiences and improve its decision-making over time, just as a human who learns from day to day would.

That is why those developing artificial intelligence are taking steps in that direction, exploring the ability of models to remember, adapt and refine their behaviour based on previous interactions. When this technology matures, humanoid robots will cease to be mere executors of commands and become intelligent machines capable of operating in the real world with true autonomy.

🎼The importance of orchestrators

So far, we have talked about intelligent agents in terms of this ability to take control of a task in order to execute it, but the usual case will be for multiple agents to work in a coordinated way to carry out a more complete task, and this is where the concept of the orchestrator comes into play. Its main function is to organize the workflow among these agents, ensuring that each one plays its role within a broader AI ecosystem.

In the case of robotics, we have likened the agent to the brain of the humanoid robot, but in more advanced scenarios, where multiple robots or systems work together, this new key piece, the orchestrator, comes into play.

The orchestrator is not a simple task manager within an agent, but a coordinator of multiple intelligent agents working autonomously, ensuring that their actions complement each other and that individual decisions contribute to a common goal.

For example, in a factory with several humanoid robots, each equipped with its own intelligent agent, the orchestrator would be in charge of distributing tasks, avoiding redundancies and ensuring that the robots collaborate efficiently.

This kind of coordination is essential in complex environments such as logistics, healthcare or even infrastructure maintenance, where multiple agents must operate in parallel without interference.

As we move toward a model in which humanoid robots will work side by side with humans and other autonomous systems, orchestrators will be necessary to ensure that collaboration among them is smooth, efficient and aligned with the goals that have been set for them.

♾️We need the arrival of AGI

Full autonomy for the robot will arrive when it has a greater capacity to reason, and for that Artificial General Intelligence, AGI to its friends, will be necessary. This is what I have learned writing this article: levels of autonomy depend not only on the decision-making capacity we grant the robot, but above all on its level of intelligence. We could give it all the autonomy in the world, but there would be many situations in which it would get stuck, because it does not have the capacity for improvisation, initiative or reaction that we people have; simply put, it is not yet that intelligent.

In other words, a humanoid robot in our home or in a factory will be as useful as the artificial intelligence installed in it allows. In everyday life there will be many situations to which it will know how to react, but there will also be others where it has no equivalent of an answer, and there human intervention will be necessary. Seen this way, it is not so different from what happens with people: depending on their personality and training, some people have a greater capacity for action and autonomy in all kinds of situations, and others are more passive or find it harder to react.

That is why, before talking about full autonomy in robots, we will first have to solve the challenge of AGI. Currently, the most advanced robots can execute specific tasks with great precision, adapt to certain changes in their environment and even make limited decisions based on data and previous patterns. However, they lack what really defines human intelligence: the ability to reason in any context, improvise novel solutions and act on their own initiative without depending on predefined rules.

AGI represents the threshold at which an AI will not only respond to programmed or trained stimuli, but will be able to learn continuously, generate its own goals and understand the world in a way similar to how we humans do. Without AGI, any robot, however advanced, will continue to operate within the limits of its programming and training. It will be autonomous in many everyday situations, but it will get stuck in others that require creativity, intuition or moral judgment.

Only when we achieve an artificial intelligence capable of generalizing knowledge, learning in real time and making decisions with a high degree of independence will we be able to speak of true full autonomy in robots. Until then, autonomy will remain relative: a combination of what we humans allow it to do and what its AI allows it to understand.

This makes Sam Altman's obsession with AGI much easier to understand. I sense that it will not happen overnight, as happened with GenAI, but will really be a path to follow: if every day we manage to make Artificial Intelligence a little more intelligent, one day we will realize that it is doing many of the things it could not do before, and that is what will give humanoid robots total usefulness.

💥The future of super robots

Following this line of reasoning, what prevents us from giving robots superpowers? So far, manufacturers are taking care that they do not have characteristics that could pose a danger to people; for example, if the robot falls on a child, it could hurt them badly. But there is no doubt that robots could be equipped with sensors, tools and capabilities that far exceed those of humans.

This is a reflection that will have to be made when the time comes, but until then, current technology already allows these machines to do many things for which we humans are not prepared. For example, at the presentation of Grok 3, we heard Elon Musk say that he will send Tesla's Optimus robots to Mars. These robots do not need to breathe and their materials can be prepared to be radiation-resistant. So problem solved: space exploration is going to benefit greatly from the use of Humanoid Robots, and that is very good for everyone.
