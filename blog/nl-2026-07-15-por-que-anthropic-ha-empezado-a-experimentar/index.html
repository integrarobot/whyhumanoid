Why has Anthropic started experimenting with robots?

The creators of Claude want their AI to transcend the virtual world into the physical world, and they are already testing it for controlling robots.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 15 July 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

Over this summer, scientists at the company Anthropic, creators of Claude, have renewed their interest in robotics and are starting to make very interesting progress that could represent a new boost for the arrival of general-purpose robotics.

Pay attention to this, which they published on 18 June:

"We observe a pattern in which, first, models help humans. Then, humans help models. Finally, models are able to perform tasks by themselves. We have seen this before in cybersecurity, and now the same dynamic is starting to take shape at the intersection of AI and the physical world."

The cybersecurity point is important because this year Anthropic has had enormous prominence worldwide due to the development of the Mythos model, which has turned the entire IT security sector upside down with its ability to discover software vulnerabilities much more efficiently than people. This has even led the company to have political problems, with the United States' temporary ban on the Fable model, the general-public version of Mythos.

If the creators of Claude really achieve advances as important in applying AI to robotics as they have in cybersecurity, this will be a great boost for the development of Physical AI, which is why it is so important for us to know in detail what they have been doing and how it could evolve.

🗓️ August 2025: Project Fetch begins, an experiment to test to what extent Claude could help Anthropic employees, who were not robotics experts, perform sophisticated tasks with a quadruped robot, and they found that being able to use the state-of-the-art model at the time (Claude Opus 4.1) allowed one team to far outperform the other, which could only rely on the Internet and its own ingenuity. The team that had Claude's help managed to do more, and faster.

🗓️ June 2026: Project Fetch is resumed to see whether the most recent models could outperform the previous generation. Not only did they succeed, but Claude Opus 4.7, operating without human assistance, was about 20 times faster than the fastest human team on all the tasks completed by participants in the experiment less than a year earlier.

As a conclusion of this Anthropic work, what we find is that generalist AI models used in robotics are improving rapidly, but their capability depends largely on the control method they use. When they have to control the joints themselves, they tend to fail. However, when they supervise a pre-trained controller or use simple guidance tools, they can complete real navigation and manipulation tasks. Some forms of robotics remain difficult to handle and control, but the most recent models are much more effective at adjusting their strategies and turning visual and sensory understanding into appropriate actions across different domains.

This is important for the development and safe deployment of language models in robotics. Current frontier models cannot control humanoid robots without a pre-trained policy, but the most recent models have made significant progress in direct manipulation and high-level policy control in humanoid and quadruped embodiments.

Foundation models are beginning to be able to orchestrate robotic capabilities that already exist, so the agent's value will lie not in generating every movement, but in:

  • Understanding human intention
  • Breaking down a task
  • Choosing the right robotic capability
  • Supervising its execution
  • Detecting errors
  • Changing strategy
  • Learning from previous attempts

So what this Anthropic work is teaching us is that in the future the usual way of working in robotics will have the following structure:

Person → AI agent → VLA → controller → robot

But more importantly, what is going to happen in robotics is also going to happen in technology in general; that is, AI Agents are going to become the interface through which we interact with technology. What until now we have done through software or websites to manage things, communicate or get information, we will now do through Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, … in natural language, without buttons, menus, links, … all through words, whether written or spoken. And it will be those agents that take charge of managing software for us or generating it "on the fly" if necessary.

Mistral is also keen on robotics

Now that we know the latest Anthropic is doing in robotics, which we can add to the recent news of OpenAI searching for robotics engineers and the work DeepMind is doing in Physical AI to build the Android of robotics, let's also look at how other AI labs are setting their sights on robotics as one of the next milestones on their path.

Specifically, in Europe we have Mistral as the main LLM developer, and it is now also setting out to create its own autonomous navigation model for robots.

Robostral Navigate allows robots to navigate autonomously in complex environments using only an RGB camera, achieving 76.6% success on unseen R2R-CE benchmarks and outperforming multi-sensor approaches. The model was developed entirely by Mistral with simulated data and token optimization techniques; it generalizes to different types of robots and adapts to real-world obstacles not seen during training. The model combines pointer-based navigation with reinforcement learning for continuous improvement, paving the way toward AI embedded in robotics.

As you can imagine, all these joint efforts by AI labs, added to those being made by robotics companies, are a new catalyst that will further accelerate the development of Physical AI.
