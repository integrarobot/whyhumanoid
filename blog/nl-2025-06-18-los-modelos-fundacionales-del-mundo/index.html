Foundation models of the world

Training Physical AI requires data through world models

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 18 June 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

When we talk about robot software, we should distinguish between three types (for now) that allow, or will allow in the future, these machines to end up fulfilling the full function we expect of them. Namely:

⚙️ The control software that allows the robot to move and fulfil the function assigned to it. Here we find notable initiatives such as ROS as a framework for developing robot software. 👁️ The VLA that gives it intelligence, which is the equivalent of LLMs for conversational assistants, although in this case it is more like a multimodal model, since it incorporates vision and language capabilities, adding action as a third component. 🌍 The world model, to which we devote this article, which is used to train the robot, allowing it to understand how the physical world works (friction, gravity, temperature, humidity, distance, …) in which it will have to perform its function, with the help of the previous two.

So let's talk about world models, because the newest piece of news is that Meta has entered this business by launching the V-JEPA 2 model.

Namely, V-JEPA 2 is a world model with a predictive architecture that achieves state-of-the-art performance in visual understanding and prediction in the physical world. A model that has been trained on videos with 1.2 billion parameters and that can be used for robot planning in "zero-shot" mode to interact with unfamiliar objects in new environments.

Pay attention, because this is how the Meta AI team, led by Yann LeCun, explains why world models are necessary for robots to manage in real life in the same way an agent does in the digital world:

We all know that if you throw a tennis ball into the air, gravity will pull it down. It would be surprising if it floated, suddenly spun in mid-air and flew off in another direction, or spontaneously turned into an apple. That kind of physical intuition is not something adults acquire after years of education; young children develop this intuition by observing the world around them even before they can form complete sentences. The ability to predict how the world will respond to our actions (or those of others) is something we humans use constantly, especially when planning what actions to take and how to best handle new situations. Our internal model of the world gives us this intuition and also acts as an internal simulator, allowing us to predict the outcome of a hypothetical action, so that we can ultimately choose the best action according to what we believe will allow us to achieve our goal. Before acting, we use our world model to imagine the possible consequences.

Therefore, the world model developed by Meta gives robots the following capabilities:

🧠 Understanding: being able to understand observations of the world, including things such as recognizing objects, actions and movements in a video. 🔮 Prediction: being able to make predictions about how the world will evolve and how it will change if the agent takes an action. 🗺️ Planning: thanks to the ability to make predictions, a world model should be useful for planning sequences of actions that make it possible to achieve a given goal.

It is also interesting to look at Meta's future plans with JEPA models, since they want to focus on training hierarchical models capable of learning, reasoning and planning across multiple temporal and spatial scales. Another important direction will be multimodal models that can make predictions using various senses, such as sight, hearing and touch.

This sounds a lot like what VLAs are, so we see that Meta is determined to go all out with the development of AI software for robots. A strategy quite similar to the one adopted by NVIDIA, which has Cosmos as its world model and Isaac GR00T for training humanoid robots with AI.

1X's world model

Once again this week we have to talk about 1X, since after presenting its VLA called Redwood, it is in the news again for presenting its world model, which is meant to serve as a bridge between atoms and bits.

The world model is a digital twin of the real world that predicts the future consequences of the robot's actions. Thanks to the use of a deep generative neural network, a whole lifetime can be compressed into one second.

This world model created by 1X seeks to tackle the complexity of the real world by learning directly from recordings of thousands of hours of robot interaction captured from raw sensory data. This makes it possible to model complex physical interactions such as grasping delicate objects, anticipating collisions between rigid bodies and the deformation of objects when they are manipulated, which gives the model a deep understanding of how the physics of the world around us works.

As training and the corresponding data collection increase, especially those moments when the robot fails to execute the task, the quality of the model can be seen to improve progressively. In this way, the company acknowledges that the most valuable data for this training is the data obtained by the robot itself when it faces performing a task autonomously, even if that task initially fails.

The world model developed by 1X continuously visualizes future states, which can be used as a cognitive core for the robot in order to plan and dynamically adjust its actions based on predicted outcomes. This predictive capability allows Neo to execute tasks more successfully and adapt better to unexpected events, which will gradually allow it to become a general-purpose and truly intelligent robot.

Spatial Intelligence and Fei-Fei Li's work at World Labs

In 2007 Fei-Fei Li led the development of ImageNet, an enormous visual database designed to drive object recognition. The project involved labelling more than 14 million images with Amazon Mechanical Turk and inspired the ILSVRC – ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge, which drove progress in deep learning and generated drastic improvements in image classification performance. The database tackled a key obstacle in computer vision: the lack of large labelled datasets for training machine learning models. Today, ImageNet is considered a key innovation underpinning advances in autonomous vehicles, facial recognition and medical imaging.

In fact, the winner of the ILSVRC challenge in 2012 was AlexNet, with the participation of Alex Krizhevsky (Google), Geoffrey Hinton (Google) and Ilya Sutskever (Google and OpenAI), an initiative that NVIDIA cites as an example of the beginning of the use of GPUs for the development of Generative Artificial Intelligence.

Almost 20 years after ImageNet's contribution to the development of computer vision technologies, Fei-Fei Li is back on the attack, in this case to drive spatial intelligence, which will allow machines to understand and interact with the world around them. We humans use spatial intelligence to create images in our minds and give them form in the physical world. We use it to reason, move and invent: to visualize and design anything, from humble sandcastles to towering cities.

Fei-Fei Li's current work with World Labs aims to go one step beyond the capabilities of LLMs, toward the development of an AI with spatial intelligence that can model the world and reason about objects, places and interactions in 3D space and time.

World Labs' world generation model has not yet shown applications in robotics, but there is no doubt that it fits very well with everything we have described here about foundation models of the world, in a strategy very similar to the one developed decades ago with ImageNet, so we will have to keep a close eye on its development.

Sam Altman predicts we will have humanoid robots in 2027

Sam Altman has expanded at length on his blog about the development of superintelligence and says that, although robots are not yet walking the streets, we have built systems that are smarter than people in many ways and capable of significantly amplifying the performance of those who use them. Pay attention to his predictions for the future:

🧠 2025: we will reach agents that can do real cognitive work

🧪 2026: we will probably reach systems capable of discovering new knowledge

🦾 2027: we may see the arrival of robots that can perform tasks in the real world

"*In the 2030s, intelligence and energy — ideas, and the ability to make them happen — will be wildly abundant. These two have been the fundamental limiters of human progress for a long time; with abundant intelligence and energy (and good governance), we can theoretically have anything else*"
