🖐️ Why is the robot's hand so important?

Let's take a moment to understand how the design and manufacture of hands for humanoid robots is evolving.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 9 April 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Unlike most robots, which are equipped with two-finger grippers, humanoids like Neo are equipped with five fingers that move much like the human hand

This is how The New York Times describes the advances in 1X's robot as far as its hand is concerned, and this has led me to think about how relevant this part of robots is for meeting expectations regarding the usefulness they can offer us in all kinds of circumstances.

Just yesterday, while taking part in an event, I met the founder of the Spanish company Lirio Bionics, which is precisely devoted to developing robotic hands, but in this case to be used as prostheses for humans rather than as robot hands. From his presentation I would like to highlight the progression he showed us regarding why this type of technology can come to fulfil a function that offers so much usefulness.

Starting with its application in health and medicine, where medical professionals now have a great ally in their drive to improve people's lives. Then he showed us its usefulness in defence, with a truly spectacular result, since bomb disposal experts can operate a robotic hand from a distance using virtual reality glasses connected to their own hands, which completely eliminates the physical risk to people. Finally we came to examples from everyday life, and this is where we will notice the greatest progress in the coming years, since more and more people are racking their brains to find all kinds of uses for this technology.

In this regard, it is worth saying that robotic hands have been the subject of research and development for decades; in fact, those of us who saw a whole trend emerge in educational robotics regarded this component as the most sophisticated one in the world of robots. I would also like to mention that in 2016, when I first wrote about robots in my previous newsletter Futurizable, robotic hands were in full development boom, so now, with even more reason, we have to look at what is happening in this respect regarding humanoid robots.

So let's look at some examples of robotic hand developments, both by robot manufacturers and by research centres.

  • Unitree is one of the companies becoming most popular, having already put its small humanoid robot on sale and taking part in all kinds of events that bring it a lot of visibility. As far as the robot's hand is concerned, it has just presented significant advances in dexterity.
  • Tesla has also boasted quite a bit about the manual manipulation capabilities of its Optimus robot. In fact, at the We Robot event a lot of time was devoted to showing how the robots were able to work as waiters, and Elon Musk mentions whenever he can that it is the most sophisticated hand on the market.
  • Sanctuary, for its part, has recently announced significant advances in the tactile sensitivity of the hand of its Phoenix humanoid robot. The improvements in the new tactile sensors allow robots to perform complex touch-driven tasks with precision and accuracy.

But it is not only companies that design robotic hands; there is a great deal of research on the subject that has surely made it possible for us to now be talking about humanoid robots and to begin to sense that they will soon be manufactured by the millions to perform all these tasks we expect them to do for us.

  • NASA calls Robonaut the robotic hand it has created together with General Motors. With its anthropomorphic design, it manages to perform a set of useful grasps for working with human interfaces. The key to the hand's performance lies in the use of lower-friction drive elements and in the redistribution of components from the hand to the forearm, which allows more sensors to be placed in the fingers and palm, where they are most needed.
  • ORCA is an open-source, reliable and cost-effective anthropomorphic robotic hand for uninterrupted learning of dexterous tasks. Its creators explain that general-purpose robots must possess human-like dexterity and agility to match human versatility; however, the obstacle to dexterous manipulation lies not only in software but possibly even more in hardware. Hence they are developing this project collaboratively.
  • DEXHAND is the robotic hand developed by the DLR research centre for use in space missions, able to withstand the conditions of space (temperature, vacuum, radioactivity, lubricants, vibration and impact loads). They developed it together with ESA, researching the use of telemanipulated systems on the International Space Station (ISS) to assist astronauts. In order to use the tools developed for astronauts, it is the size of a glove for extravehicular activities.

Increasingly skilful and sensitive hands

As you can see, just as is happening with humanoid robots in general, their hands are becoming increasingly skilful and sensitive. To this we should add the robot's own artificial intelligence, which offers it ever greater usefulness.

Thus we find that, in order to keep advancing, work is being done through three different approaches:

1️⃣ High-precision, high-complexity hands that replicate human anatomy and movements with great fidelity; they integrate motors, artificial tendons, and tactile and force sensors; use impedance control and machine learning; and are used in research, space exploration, surgery and advanced robotics.

2️⃣ Low-cost functional hands with open, collaborative design; made with 3D printing, servomotors and simple controllers; ideal for education, prototyping and service robotics, all through the promotion of open innovation and open source.

3️⃣ Hands based on soft robotics and intelligent pneumatic actuators, such as those developed by the company Festo for the BionicSoftHand, which are gaining ground, showing that not all parts of a robot need to be rigid and metallic.

More human, autonomous and versatile hands

And as for the future, here is a preview of how hand technology for humanoid robots may evolve:

🔬 Extreme miniaturization: when their developers are able to fit more actuators and sensors into less space.

👆 Hyper-realistic touch: artificial skins with distributed sensors and advanced haptics. Hence robots will gradually look more human.

🧠 Cognitive control: hands that not only obey orders, but understand contexts, learn from experience and act autonomously.

🪶 Lighter, faster and more energy-efficient hands: to work in mobile robots, prostheses or exoskeletons without depending on cables or large batteries.

🤝 Fluid interaction with humans: so they can collaborate naturally, safely and understandably in all kinds of environments, from a factory to a shop and a home.

And to finish, a reflection: if you were a humanoid robot manufacturer, where would you put your main efforts? As a first option we have mechanics, like what we have seen with hands, or we could also bet everything on intelligence, so that they better understand the world they inhabit. Humanoid robot companies must undoubtedly be under great pressure when deciding where to put their resources and time.
