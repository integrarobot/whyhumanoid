Robots that save lives

In this newsletter we look at the interesting case of robots working in the field of people's health and well-being.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 21 January 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

When we talk about robots, the most discussed use case is performing manual work because of labour shortages, a need that is evident and increasingly urgent for many economies. But we don't usually mention the aspect of precision, or how convenient it can be for a machine to carry out a certain task much more effectively than a human can.

Let's look, for example, at autonomous driving, where it has been shown that accidents fall significantly when we remove the human factor from behind the wheel and let the machine make the decisions, basically because it doesn't get distracted, doesn't fall asleep, doesn't drive drunk and can have a broader view of the whole.

We could transfer the same to other functions, for example those we are going to devote this article to, related to health. Because indeed, the problem of distraction or lack of lucidity due to overwork also occurs among healthcare professionals. Hence, in part, the problem of iatrogenesis, or the harm a patient may suffer from a certain treatment because of some kind of negligence or problem that may have occurred in the hospital.

Automation is also a solution for the future in this field, but here we must also add the staff shortage, because if we talk about healthcare professionals, many countries also lack them, and although it is an aspirational profession, it can also happen that we don't have as many doctors and nurses as we would like.

Note: no criticism of healthcare professionals, only of their bosses who decide they should work 24-hour shifts and other outrages of that kind.

The case of Vitestro's robot as an example of what is to come

Vitestro is a great example of what a professional future in which humans coexist with robots will be like. In this case you can see how healthcare staff carry out a role of decision-making, care and accompanying patients, but the machine is the one in charge of the technical work.

I have been following the adventures of this startup, founded in the Netherlands in 2017, for some time, and I was very pleased to see that they have now launched their commercial product, the autonomous blood-drawing robot Aletta.

Aletta has been designed to perform the complete blood draw procedure autonomously, which includes identifying the right vein, inserting the needle, changing and inverting the tubes and applying the bandage. To locate the vein it uses multimodal imaging combining near-infrared and ultrasound. In addition, Doppler ultrasound is used to confirm that it has selected a vein and not an artery. The robot then positions and inserts the needle with submillimetre precision.

By automating these steps, the aim is to improve consistency and reduce variability in blood draws, which supports the transition toward fully automated laboratory workflows.

And I say this is a good example of what a future in which we live with robots will be like because in Aletta's presentation video you can see perfectly what the human's role is relative to the machine.

Something quite similar happens with the Da Vinci robot, developed by the medical robotics pioneer Intuitive, which has been used for many years to improve doctors' precision in surgical procedures. The robot is a complement to the human, who in this case, thanks to very advanced technologies, can improve the precision of the procedure.

In most cases, when we talk about robotics in healthcare, the person will have to carry out a role of patient care, process supervision, answering questions, providing confidence, ... in the end, the human touch we all seek when facing something as critical as our health.

Namely, the startup Vitestro has raised nearly 50 million in funding, so it has significant resources to bring its technology to market, and it also has the support, as investor and advisor, of Fred Moll, who is the mind behind the Da Vinci robot and who now devotes himself to driving other medical robotics companies.

The health sector is undoubtedly one of those that can benefit most from robotics, not only by improving the precision of interventions, but also by increasing the efficiency of procedures, reducing as far as possible the bottlenecks that mean many people cannot be properly cared for when they turn to the healthcare system.

How robotics is currently being applied in the health sector

  1. Surgical robots that assist doctors: we have already talked about Intuitive here, but it is worth developing a little, because precision is fundamental when we talk about surgery. Just as other kinds of professionals rely on machines such as microscopes for tasks related to science or electronics, in medicine significant efforts have been made to apply these technologies to improve surgical operations. Another example of a company working in this activity is Vicarious Surgical, which has developed a robotic surgical system focused on abdominal access and visualization through a single 1.5 cm incision for inserting the camera and two robotic instruments, helping surgeons perform operations with much greater visibility and manoeuvrability, carrying out minimally invasive surgery. Other companies such as CMR Surgical with its Versius robot or Medtronic with Hugo RAS follow the same trend: modular, portable and more affordable robots that seek to bring robot-assisted surgery to many more hospitals. They all share the idea of giving the surgeon more control, improving ergonomics and reducing surgical trauma.
  2. Assistive robots that accompany patients. We have already talked about this topic before, and it is one of those producing the most tangible short-term results. Here I refer you to the article I published about social robots, specifically mentioning the cases of Mirokaï, from the company Enchanted, which is used in hospitals to accompany children in radiotherapy rooms; the Abi robot from the company Andromeda, which is used to help aged care teams in Australia bring joy and connection to residents; and Paro, a seal-shaped therapeutic robot that takes part in cognitive stimulation programmes, holding short conversations, leading group activities and comforting shy patients. Of course, these robots don't cure diseases, but they do improve the patient experience by offering company and distraction, aspects that are often fundamental to recovery.
  3. Mobile robots that transport objects in hospitals. Here we must highlight Moxi, the robot created by Diligent Robotics for collaborative work, which performs logistics functions that relieve hospital staff. Moxi has already surpassed one million deliveries in US hospitals, carrying out tasks such as taking supplies from the pharmacy to the ward. The robot is controlled through an app: staff request a delivery, the order is assigned in the cloud and Moxi executes it, even if the connection is interrupted. Thanks to its wheel system, a robotic arm and its cameras, it can navigate the environment, interact with lifts and indicate to humans where it is heading. According to the company itself, this fleet of robots has saved staff more than 1.5 billion steps and 575,000 hours of work, something that allows professionals to spend more time with patients and less walking corridors. Another example is TUG, from the company Aethon, which consists of a robot that works like an autonomous cart, designed to transport medication, lab samples, surgical supplies and even waste. These vehicles are used in more than 37 hospitals in the US Veterans Affairs network and make it possible to increase the efficiency of nursing, pharmacy and laboratory services.
  4. Exoskeletons would not directly fall into the robot category, but they are so interesting that it is worth talking about them in this health-related article. There is no doubt that the advances we have seen in this area in recent years are spectacular, so it is interesting to know some of the companies working in this field. This is the case of the ReWalk personal exoskeleton, developed by Lifeward (formerly ReWalk Robotics), which allows people with spinal cord injuries to stand up, walk and climb stairs. The device, which is already covered by Medicare, offers control functions with crutches and a smartwatch that makes it easy to select walking modes. The company states that regular use of the exoskeleton improves spasticity, trunk control, bowel and urinary function and mental health. The company Wandercraft also works on developing exoskeletons, and better still, they have created a kind of evolution toward a hybrid system with a humanoid robot that can work autonomously or be used as a platform for a person with reduced mobility.

So you see, we don't just have surgical robots; Physical AI and robotics are beginning to have great applications in health. Who knows, perhaps one day we will even see Optimus working as a doctor in some hospital, and the prediction recently made by Elon Musk will come true.
