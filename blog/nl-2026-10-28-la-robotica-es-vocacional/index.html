Robotics is a vocation

This is the reason why many entrepreneurs did not throw in the towel in the early days of robotics, even though they did not have AI as developed as it is now.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 28 October 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

I was reading the story of the French robotics startup Wandercraft and thought it was worth sharing because I think it reflects very well the vocation many entrepreneurs who decide to devote themselves to robotics have.

This is how the company itself tells it:

It all began with a question a young engineer couldn't get out of his head. At a university robotics club, Nicolas Simon watched a machine learn to walk and thought of his brother, who was losing that ability. If a robot can walk, why not create one that walks for him? Wandercraft was founded in Paris in 2012 to answer that question, and the answer required a decade of engineering: an exoskeleton that balances itself, so that the person wearing it doesn't have to. In 2019, Atalante became the first self-balancing walking exoskeleton to obtain CE marking in Europe. That same technology today accompanies people in their rehabilitation in more than 150 clinical and research centres, leaves the clinic to enter the home with Eve, and is present in Calvin, our family of humanoid robots designed for the toughest jobs in industry. Three products, two markets, one conviction: robotics must help people achieve what they cannot do alone.

The robotics club Nicolas Simon was part of when he came up with the idea of creating an exoskeleton is not an exception, since there is no doubt that universities have, over the years, created the ideal breeding ground for many situations like this to arise. And though I'm not a fan of formal education, in this case we have to acknowledge the merit of those teachers and researchers who decided at the time to create this kind of space for students to experiment with small robots and other kinds of technology, such as Arduino, 3D printers, drones, … which has ended up leading many of those students to seek to fulfil the ultimate dream of every robot fan: humanoids.

How many more of the hundreds of entrepreneurs currently working on developing humanoids follow Nicolas Simon's pattern? I dare say many; what's more, every day that passes, the number of people who may develop that vocation increases as the concept of the general-purpose robot becomes known and studied, at universities and in a self-taught way.

But beyond vocation being behind many entrepreneurs' decision to set out to create new companies in this sector, what is beyond doubt is that there are enough incentives to want to work in robotics, because as society advances it becomes clearer that many of the jobs people now do should be done by a robot.

In my talks I usually use the example of the plough. Before tractors, the farmer had to exert force on the plough to get it to enter the soil. The horse or ox did the pulling, but the farmer was not exempt from having to make a great physical effort, something we would now consider inhumane.

Image: Image

So I think that over time we will transfer this same situation to many other jobs that many people now have to do because they have no choice. And among those jobs, I think those that involve a person standing in front of a conveyor belt performing a repetitive task are among the first that should be delegated to a humanoid. That is precisely the use case Wandercraft is showing us in its collaboration with Renault.

The Renault use case seems quite representative to me because it puts on the table the problem robotics wants to solve. Because Wandercraft's founder wanted to solve the problem of someone who already had a mobility problem, but what he has ended up doing is creating a machine that can prevent many people from ending up injured from having to perform repetitive work throughout a whole working day, not just one day, but repeatedly like that for years.

Why is it so important that robotics be a vocation?

Robotics is harder than AI. In fact, if it weren't for AI, I dare say we wouldn't be talking about robots now.

At this point I am going to venture an idea about which I may be wrong but which may reflect quite well the difference between robotics before and after ChatGPT. Unitree's own CEO put it this way recently: the biggest mistake I have made in the last decade has been not believing enough in AI. And what I saw of robotics before the arrival of Generative AI was a very different strategy from the one we see being pursued now, basically because the focus was on programming robots or developing a type of deterministic AI, but in both cases progress was slow and ended up in a dead end.

Because the robots we really need must be general-purpose, exactly as with AI. Because AI also advanced slowly before the arrival of transformers, and the good news for all those who have spent many years devoted to robotics is that it is being shown that what works for AI also works for robotics.

That is exactly what Jim Fan, NVIDIA's director of robotics, expressed recently when he said: now you too can feel the Physical Bitter Lesson at home, as we did on that fateful day ;)

He is referring to the fact that the bitter lesson of Artificial Intelligence is also being fulfilled in Physical AI, that is, the greater the volume of data and the greater the computing capacity, the better the results, even if the algorithm has not improved.

So those who got into robotics years ago, when everything seemed impossible and it was vocation that allowed them to stand firm in the face of earlier difficulties, now have a clear path to advance, because it seems increasingly clear that the optimal conditions for it are in place.
