2026, the year mass production of humanoid robots will begin

Making predictions is as risky as it is fun, so today I dare to take stock of a year devoted to robotics and imagine what next year could be like.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 17 December 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

A year devoted to studying humanoid robots gives a lot to talk about, and now that 2025 is ending it is a good time to take stock of what has happened with Physical AI and the development of general-purpose robots. So here is my list of the most important milestones that have taken place in this industry and how I think this sector could evolve next year.

  1. Humanoid robots leave the laboratories.
  2. VLA models start to be developed.
  3. We need world models as soon as possible.
  4. NVIDIA seeks to repeat its AI play with robotics.
  5. No tech giant wants to be left out.
  6. Tesla in a period of transition and Optimus in question.
  7. First billion-dollar rounds in the industry.
  8. 1X, the first Western startup to commercialize its robot.
  9. Unitree: a robotics laboratory in the real world.

Let's go through all this point by point, to better understand what is to come and how we can prepare to take advantage of this big trend.

1. Humanoid robots leave the laboratories.

This year robot manufacturers have been daring to take them out of the laboratory. The best proof of this was the humanoid robot olympics held in China in August. We have also seen many robots at tech fairs, but the acid test has been the appearances of some Tesla and Figure robots at events. On this point, the issue of teleoperation should be highlighted. It is clear that robot manufacturers want their robots to be autonomous, but for safety reasons for now they prefer to teleoperate them. Here we should add that they are not alone in facing the challenge; many companies are working exclusively on developing Physical AI.

2. VLA models start to be developed.

The focus of robot software development has been on vision, language and action models, that is, an evolution of LLMs that have the ability to interact with the real world. At this point it should be made clear that this year there have been enormous advances in improving the reliability of generative AI models thanks to the development of reasoning models and intelligent agents, which has a direct impact on the availability of better AI models for robots. It should also be highlighted here that it is increasingly clear that training robots will require a large amount of video data, which will be used in reinforcement learning, to later refine machine learning with corrections made by humans.

3. We need world models as soon as possible.

Although the companies that make humanoid robots have not talked much about it, there seems to be a certain consensus among highly renowned scientists in the AI world, such as Fei-Fei Li and Yan LeCun, regarding the need to take one more step in the development of artificial intelligence for robots. This step would consist of providing machines with information about the physical structure of the real world so that they can better understand how to interact with it. On this they agree with NVIDIA, which has been working for some time on developing its world foundation model called Cosmos.

4. NVIDIA seeks to repeat its AI play with robotics.

I can't get inside Jensen Huang's head, but I would like to interpret that his phrase repeated at events, "*the time for robots has come*", is something he has fully internalized and is what he really wants to happen, not just part of the script of what he has to say on stage. Since the risk that it is just marketing is there, we should look at what he is actually doing, and the truth is that everything looks very good: in addition to presenting the Jetson Thor AI chip for robots, he has made great progress in developing software such as Isaac and Cosmos, and it has also become known that he will start taking his own medicine, incorporating robots in the factories where Foxconn manufactures some of his products.

5. No tech giant wants to be left out.

If putting skin in the game can be done with money, many large companies have shown, by investing in startups, that they really believe in this business and are willing to bet a lot of money on it. And best of all, here we are not talking only about the Bigtech companies led by NVIDIA or major investment funds such as Softbank; many electronics, household appliance and automotive companies are also placing their bets on this technology. And the best thing about all this is that these investors not only contribute money, but also have a lot of experience in mass manufacturing, which right now is one of the biggest challenges for this industry.

6. Tesla in a period of transition and Optimus in question.

Image: Image

They say the road to success is paved with small failures, and this is something Elon Musk's companies know very well. For example, we should look here at the number of times SpaceX had to crash its rockets before it managed to land them so they could be reused for new launches. This is something nobody dared to do; I suppose it was considered impossible, but now that they have achieved it, other companies such as Blue Origin have shown that it was not an exception. The same should happen with the Optimus robots: all these manufacturing delays and malfunctions in public will surely become great lessons for the future that Tesla will know how to capitalize on.

7. First billion-dollar rounds in the industry.

What can be said about the rain of millions that has fallen on companies manufacturing humanoid robots, and above all on those developing Physical AI software. Although in the AI sector talent is more decisive than budget, there is no doubt that attracting AI geniuses requires a lot of money; just ask Mark Zuckerberg. So when it comes to funding robotics startups, the prize goes to Figure, with 1.9 billion dollars raised and a valuation of 39 billion dollars.

8. 1X, the first Western startup to commercialize its robot.

We don't yet know whether this could be considered the ChatGPT moment of robotics; it all depends on whether 1X fulfils its commitment to start delivering robots in 2026 and, above all, on what the experience is like when they reach homes. But what is clear is that it is a great milestone for a company to have decided to commercialize its product even though there is still so much uncertainty about it. Basically, the company needed real-world data to train its robots, and the most direct way to get it is by putting its robots on the market. As Tesla did with cars, but in this case having to use professionals who teleoperate the robots for those situations where the machine is unable to perform the task assigned to it.

9. Unitree: a robotics laboratory in the real world.

One of the most pleasant surprises I have had this year in robotics is seeing how a large number of robotics scientists choose to give Unitree's G1 robot usefulness instead of continuing down the path of trying to create their own humanoid robot. I mean that until this year what we saw in research centres were attempts to create the complete machine, but now what we find is that many people have decided that, since they can access a robot whose hardware side is fairly well solved, what is worthwhile is to advance in software development. For my part, I have compiled in the humanoid robot database a list of 21 research projects that give Unitree's robot usefulness, which will help encourage more and more progress toward having a truly functional low-cost robot.

2026: the year of mass production of humanoid robots.

Having taken stock, let's move on to the predictions, always risky but fun to make and to read.

When I say mass production I mean millions, not thousands, and since I'm counting on China, I don't think I'm going too far. What I'm not so sure about is that this production will be destined for individuals. I see so much need in companies that I imagine they will be willing to pay much more for a robot than a family will.

Although my experience is mainly with small companies, I also see a lot of interest from large companies, which until now had focused on robotic arms but are starting to see that humanoids can better solve other situations. In fact, just today I was told about the big bet Inditex wants to make on this technology, so it is clear that the market demands it and that manufacturers will have to be able to respond to this need.

---

That said, I'm signing off for a few weeks. I hope you can join me in 2026 to see whether my prediction comes true; until then, all that remains is to wish you a merry Christmas and a very successful year ahead.
