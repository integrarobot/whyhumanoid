🪫🔋 The battery problem

We interview Boris Gragera, founder of the battery company Inition, to better understand the state of development of this technology.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 5 March 2025 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Image: Image

The dream of humanoid robots working in factories 24/7 is still far off, not only because of the need for Artificial Intelligence-based software that better understands how intricate the real world is, but above all because of the current capacity of batteries, which means very short operating times for these machines.

To dig deeper into this idea, at Robots Humanoides we interviewed Boris Gragera, a Spanish entrepreneur who has founded ventures from Korea, the USA and the UK to develop what is now his company Inition, which has reinvented the way battery electrodes are manufactured. Thanks to this technology, the energy consumption of electrode production is reduced by 50% and 10 times less factory space is required. Toxic fumes are also eliminated in a scalable process that reduces the necessary capital investment by 65%.

🧑‍🔧 Being an entrepreneur in the battery business

Boris has been working in the world of batteries as an entrepreneur for 14 years. He began with the goal of improving one of the technologies used in defence and security, mainly by governments. Instead of starting a company from scratch, he found it more convenient to invest directly in a company at a very early stage and take a substantial equity stake on the condition that one of the projects would be to develop the technology he had in mind.

In a year and a half of pure research, they managed to increase the energy density of this battery used in defence and security by 35%. They tested the first prototypes with customers and, six months later, achieved a 50% improvement, which was the final goal. They then attracted great interest in their technology by taking part in the most important trade fair in this sector in the United States, but things were cut short in early 2015 when they suffered a fire at their factory in Korea and lost everything, having to start building it again from scratch.

During this time he discovered that battery production is an extremely inefficient process, especially if you want to make it accessible to different sectors of industry. It is relatively efficient if done through a gigafactory, but he believes gigafactories should not be the only way to make batteries. There must be other technologies that serve market niches, such as robotics, and that solve specific problems.

This project, which started in Korea, was later moved to New York and is finally being developed in Oxford, after a funding round that has allowed them to reach the current state of development of the technology and enter the market with their first customers.

The work Inition is doing has an enormous connection with the revolution that is going to happen with humanoid robots, since their technology will make it unnecessary to be an electrochemist, a mechanical engineer or a battery specialist in order to produce batteries. They are building the hardware, but at the same time they are incorporating artificial intelligence so that the user only has to indicate the type of market the battery is intended for and the machine will produce it.

Their technology scales from low production volumes, the opposite of what happens with gigafactories, which have to be enormous because the production technology does not scale well. But the demand for batteries that robotics will generate will be enormous when this technology is fully deployed in the market.

We have to be aware that there is a global labour shortage problem. Many people do not want to do certain jobs; basically, they do not want to be tightening screws 8 hours a day, and these tasks will be taken on by robots. The need is clear and will be covered by robotics.

As for autonomy, there is a lot of opacity about the energy consumption of these robots, which is usually a problem. One indicator we can use is that in video demonstrations you never see a robot working without cuts and interruptions. This means they have recharged the battery or even that the robot is connected by a cable. That is the problem companies like Inition want to solve.

It is impossible for all robots to use the same battery, just as not all vehicles can use the same fuel. Battery production must diversify into specific verticals. Right now, technological development is focused on hardware, and what usually happens is that at the end of the process they realize they have forgotten about the battery.

There have been surreal cases of people who design very expensive machines and then realize they did not think of leaving enough space for the battery. You have to start thinking about the robot from the battery.

That is why at Inition they currently work with robot manufacturers in Oxford, and in the UK in general, with the aim of characterizing battery consumption, charge and discharge peaks, and what type of battery will be needed for this kind of application, because there will be an enormous market when the price is acceptable, and the uses on offer will be unlimited.

🤝 On the design of current batteries for robots

The battery world must move toward customization. When it comes to manufacturing lithium-ion batteries there are multiple possible combinations, depending on the materials used in cathodes and anodes. Currently many people are waiting for the "holy grail" of batteries, but manufacturing a battery is extremely complicated, and the miracle materials that have been discovered are too expensive. There is a lot of talk about solid-state batteries, but for now it seems to be more marketing than reality; moreover, this new type of battery can at most increase battery autonomy by between 30 and 40%.

The big problem is that the battery industry and robotics currently do not work together, when they should if they want to reach an optimal state of development of this technology.

The differentiation that humanoid robot manufacturers achieve will come not only from software but also from autonomy. Right now, car manufacturers use the same batteries without differentiation, but in the future brands like Ferrari or Lamborghini will not want to use the same battery as a standard car. The same will happen in robotics.

The industry must find solutions so that a battery does not cost 100,000 euros or more. Emerging robotics companies cannot afford contracts with giants such as LG or Samsung, which won't discuss budgets of less than 20 million euros. That is why flexible and customizable production technologies must be developed. Robotics will change the world of batteries, and we are at the beginning of an enormous change.

It is essential to design robots with the battery in mind from the start. Right now there is a strong bet on software and spectacular movements, but nobody talks about the robot's real autonomy. In addition, in the current state of development of robots, in which they are being trained to function in the real world, many sensors and intensive use of software are required, which consumes much more energy than should be the case in the future when robots are already trained.

📐 Innovation from first principles and collaboration

Image: Image

How important is what Boris has told us in this interview about the need to design the robot from first principles, thinking from the start of its design about how the battery will be part of the whole, just as Tesla did when it conceived how an electric car should work to maximize its range and driving efficiency. Now, with robots, the same process must be followed, and we hope manufacturers will not neglect this critical issue.

On the other hand, we have open or collaborative innovation, especially regarding robot and battery manufacturers working in a coordinated way to find the best solutions to the problem of robot autonomy. It is to be expected that, in view of the juicy market expected for humanoid robotics, battery manufacturers will also position themselves in this segment. At least we already know that LG, with its investment in Bear Robotics, and Samsung, with 1X and Rainbow, are very interested in this opportunity.

We will therefore keep an eye on how the rest of the battery manufacturers position themselves in relation to the robotics market, as they have already done in the electric vehicle market. And we hope that initiatives like Boris Gragera's with Inition are very successful in making battery production more efficient.
