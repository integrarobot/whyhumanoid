Why is it important that the Tesla Roadster uses Optimus's actuators?

The cross-pollination between Tesla and SpaceX has once again become evident with the presentation of the Tesla Roadster, and this is also related to the Optimus robot.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 21 October 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Last week will be remembered for the presentation of Tesla's new Roadster model, a car that has gone around the world through the media, just as its predecessor has gone around the solar system after being launched on a SpaceX rocket.

That first crazy idea of Elon Musk's, deciding to launch a car into space as a kind of tribute to what this vehicle had meant for the company, has now been surpassed by making the new version of the car fly; although for now it is over short distances and it seems rather to jump like Knight Rider's car, what the car will be able to do in the future is hard to guess.

But what is not hard to sense is that this car means much more than the launch of a new product, because what it rather seems is the confirmation of the definitive alliance between Tesla and SpaceX.

The car's presentation event itself was held at SpaceX's facilities, and everything related to the propulsion that allows the car to fly comes from rockets, but what I wanted to talk about today is Optimus, which also took part, albeit discreetly, in this event.

Specifically, Optimus's contribution to the event consisted of lending part of its technology, namely the actuators that allow it to move its limbs, but in this case to move the vehicle's rear spoiler.

In this way the Tesla Roadster's spoilers use an actuator system developed for the Tesla Optimus robot, which activate Track Mode, allowing it to achieve advanced aerodynamics generating downforce close to that of Formula 1 cars.

So we are facing yet another example of the cross-pollination that exists between Tesla and SpaceX in terms of working together, but in this case not just by sharing knowledge but directly through the components that make up their products.

This cross-pollination undoubtedly has many more examples, for example in solar panels, in AI models, even in the next-generation chips they are going to build jointly through Terafab.

And why is all this I'm telling you important? Basically because the chances of a merger between Tesla and SpaceX are getting higher and higher, and this could have very important consequences for the future of the Optimus robot.

Let's think, for example, about what happened with the merger of xAI and SpaceX prior to the IPO, where the great expectation generated by the possibility of creating datacenters in space facilitated unprecedented fundraising, which now makes it much easier for SpaceX to develop its plans.

Likewise, with the presentation of the flying Tesla Roadster and this new staging of the collaboration between Tesla and SpaceX, what is becoming evident is that there is no limit to the company's possibilities and capabilities for developing technology and bringing it to market.

It is true that the deadlines Musk sets are never met and that the estimates are very exaggerated, but the reality is indisputable, for example regarding the capacity to put payload into space and create the world's largest satellite communications network.

But let's go back to Optimus, which after all is what interests us most at Integrarobot, and take the opportunity to give an update on its state of development.

Because now that it is time for Tesla to report its results, and it has filed its report for the shareholders' meeting with the SEC, more data can be learned that is very useful for knowing the state of development of Optimus. Specifically, this is what the report says:

  1. Version 3 of Optimus ready for mass production is one of the 4 milestones highlighted in the infographic with which the report opens, together with more than 10 million vehicles delivered, more than 130 GWh of storage deployed and vision-only Robotaxi in the US.
  2. Among the infrastructure investments for 2026, the construction of the first Optimus production lines is mentioned. It is mentioned in the same sentence as the Semi factory in Nevada and the new data centre capacity to train its Superintelligence systems.
  3. Among the decisions Tesla attributes to its governance structure is applying what it has learned in autonomous driving to robots, and it gives Optimus as an example, defining it as an autonomous general-purpose humanoid robot.
  4. Within the transition from a hardware-focused business to a Physical Superintelligence company, the refinement of the production-ready Optimus design is mentioned, grouped with the advances in FSD, the launch of Robotaxi and the expansion of training infrastructure.

Nothing more and nothing less. That is, we still know very little about Optimus 3, but my conclusion is that the company is no longer interested in demonstrating the robot's great capabilities, but in showing that it is able to mass-produce it, because that is really what the market needs.

What Cathie Wood thinks about the future of Optimus

Coinciding with the publication of Tesla's status report, the investment management firm ARK, led by Cathie Wood, has taken the opportunity to reformulate its valuation of the company, setting out several scenarios and including Optimus in the formula that allows it to say that in 2031 a Tesla share could be worth 4,400 dollars.

"*We believe Optimus Robot-as-a-Service (RaaS) will likely experience an inflection point as robotaxis continue to proliferate, as shown below*".

Specifically, the calculation model ARK has just published indicates that Optimus could contribute 2.29 trillion dollars to Tesla's business in 2031, which would represent 14% of the enterprise value estimated for the company.

To arrive at that figure it calculates one million robots sold cumulatively to other companies and annual revenue of 212 billion dollars. The key is that Tesla could charge a subscription for the work of those robots, so that each installed Optimus would become a source of recurring revenue that could grow as its capabilities improved.

Of course, we are talking about a forecast that depends on Tesla managing to manufacture those robots, making them work with enough autonomy and reliability and convincing companies that they are worth paying for.

In addition, it is considered that increasing computing capacity will allow Optimus to approach human performance around 2031, but that relationship still has to be demonstrated in the robot itself.

Impressive figures and incredible estimates, but what ARK says nothing about is whether we will soon see Optimus building habitats for astronauts on Mars, which is what really interests me.
