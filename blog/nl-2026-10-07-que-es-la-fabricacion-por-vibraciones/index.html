What vibe manufacturing is and what impact it will have on robotics 🏭

The popularization of manufacturing physical objects is arriving thanks to AI

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 7 October 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Appealing to the industrial revolution is quite a common resource when we want to refer to a major leap in humanity's progress. Just as we could appeal to the agricultural revolution or the Enlightenment. In the case of the AI revolution, it seems we are finally moving from the digital realm to the physical.

The thing is that here we should not give AI all the credit, since some technological advances have already occurred that serve as the foundation for the phenomenon we are going to explain today: vibe manufacturing.

Namely, just as vibe coding has popularized software development, and now anyone with an idea for a digital tool that solves a specific problem can develop it using Claude Code, Codex, Cursor or even Lovable, what we are going to argue here is that this is also starting to happen with physical products, not just digital ones.

At this point the first thing to clarify, so that people don't get alarmed, is that we are not talking about replacing the traditional but about something new that fills an existing gap. For example, if a company already has its ERP with SAP or its CRM with Salesforce, nobody, for now, is going to replace it by creating a new application from scratch with Vibe Coding.

However, what vibe programming offers is a solution for all those companies that use Excel as if it were a project management tool rather than a spreadsheet. That can indeed be done by anyone who puts in the time and the will, especially if they are clear that it is not about creating software used by millions of people, but something basic that solves a specific need that until now was unmet, poorly met or available only at a price unaffordable for that person.

That said, let's turn to the case of manufacturing, because this has very important implications for robotics, which is what concerns us here. Although there is no doubt that vibe coding will also be useful with robots, for example to create small programs that give them specific functions. But that's another story.

By the way, I'm assuming everyone knows the concept of vibe coding, but in case someone hadn't yet heard it by that name, let's take the opportunity to give credit to its creator, Andrej Karpathy:

There's a new kind of coding I call "vibe coding", where you fully give in to the vibes, embrace exponentials, and forget that the code even exists. It's possible because the LLMs are getting too good. Also, I just talk to the Artificial Intelligence, so I barely even touch the keyboard. I ask for the dumbest things like "decrease the padding on the sidebar by half" because I'm too lazy to find it. I "Accept All" always, I don't read the diffs anymore. When I get error messages I just copy paste them in with no comment, usually that fixes it. The code grows beyond my usual comprehension; I'd have to really read through it for a while. Sometimes the LLMs can't fix a bug so I just work around it or ask for random changes until it goes away. It's not too bad for throwaway weekend projects, but still quite amusing. I'm building a project or webapp, but it's not really coding - I just see stuff, say stuff, run stuff, and copy paste stuff, and it mostly works.

And now let's go back to manufacturing, and the first thing we have to ask ourselves is whether these ideas can be applied to the physical, not just the digital. The answer is yes, which is why we are talking about it at Integrarobot and why we are going to see what it is about.

But the first thing to do is clarify that this is not something totally new but rather an evolution, because, just as happened in the world of programming, it is not that you start from scratch to have something completely different; in the case of writing code there was already progress with programming frameworks that in one way or another anticipated the idea that the programmer no longer has to write all the code but rather do supervisory work. So, in the case of manufacturing, the antecedent would be the maker movement, which already did a lot to popularize the idea that anyone who was not an industrial or electronic engineer could get to work with things such as 3D printers, Arduino, Raspberry Pi, Scratch, Autocad, etc.

Although here it must be made clear that these technologies greatly facilitated access, but not so much that anyone could start manufacturing their own physical products, which is exactly what is now starting to happen with Vibe Manufacturing 🏭.

Let's see below what it is about and how it is starting to become popular.

To understand the concept, the first thing we are going to do is give an example, specifically from Nikita Bier, a serial entrepreneur who has also worked as head of product at x.com and who tells it this way:

We are now entering an era in which any product can be created exactly to the consumer's preferences and needs. I vibe-manufactured a dog door with a wifi-controlled lock, perfectly to the specifications and design of my house. I know nothing about metal fabrication or electrical engineering. It is now being manufactured and will be delivered in 2 weeks, for a cost almost the same as buying a mass-produced item straight off the shelf.

About this case, the first thing to clarify is that, as Nikita rightly says, he is manufacturing something exclusive for his house; he is not creating a new commercial product, and that is what changes the situation, because the main idea I want to convey here is that vibe manufacturing complements, it does not replace. That is, we will keep buying many things produced in large factories in massive quantities, but for very specific situations we will turn to AI, 3D printers and other kinds of machines or robots to manufacture new products 100% tailored to our needs.

💡 How to get started in vibe manufacturing in ten steps

  1. Start with something small that is useful to you. A bracket, a housing or an organizer lets you go through the whole process without the complexity of developing a complete device. If you want to sell it, talk first to those who would use it to understand what they need and how they solve it now.
  2. Measure before asking the AI for anything. Note down dimensions, fixing points, loads and available space. A digital caliper will help you with small parts, because the AI needs concrete data to design something that fits.
  3. Turn your idea into design instructions. Explain what the part must do, what objects it has to fit with and which measurements you can modify. Ask for a parametric design, that is, one you can adjust by changing values without starting over.
  4. Choose a few tools and learn to combine them. To start, an AI that generates code, such as Claude Code or Codex, and OpenSCAD let you work with parts described through instructions. If you need solid models exportable to STEP, you can explore CadQuery. Add access to a 3D printer, your own or a workshop's, to complete the journey from design to part.
  5. Prepare the file the manufacturer needs. An attractive image does not contain all the necessary information. Depending on the process, you will be asked for STEP for solid geometry, DXF for cutting profiles or STL and 3MF for 3D printing. Confirm the format, units and requirements with the supplier. CadQuery export documentation.
  6. Check that the design can be manufactured. Review thicknesses, holes, clearances and access for assembling the parts. AI can help you detect problems, but the limitations of the process and a professional's review should guide decisions when there are significant loads or risks.
  7. Make a cheap first prototype. Printing in plastic lets you check dimensions, assembly and ease of use before ordering a more expensive version. Getting it to fit is progress; its strength and durability require additional testing.
  8. Add electronics when the function justifies it. If you need sensors or connectivity, start with a development board and available components. For simple home automation projects, ESPHome lets you work with microcontrollers such as the ESP32 and integrate them with Home Assistant. Your own electronic board can wait.
  9. Test, correct and manufacture again. Use the part under the intended conditions and record what fails. Keep the design versions and the results: that history will help you understand what you have improved and what is still pending.
  10. Do the maths before turning it into a business. Include materials, manufacturing, assembly, packaging, shipping, failures and customer service. Manufacturing to order can reduce inventory, but selling also requires reviewing safety and applicable requirements. Increase quantities when you have evidence of demand and a repeatable process.

And that's all about Vibe Manufacturing 🏭, a trend that may now seem very niche but which I am convinced is going to have an enormous impact on the development of robotics. And not only robotics, because I think it is also going to be the missing piece for us to finally see the reindustrialization of the West begin. I don't think they need to worry much about the future in China, because there they will also have plenty of work, but what seems increasingly clear to me is that the situation is no longer going to be as badly distributed as it has been in recent decades.
