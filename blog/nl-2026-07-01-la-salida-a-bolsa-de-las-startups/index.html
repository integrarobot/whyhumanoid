The IPOs of robotics startups

Robotics and Physical AI startups need more money to accelerate their growth, and going public is one of the options starting to be used.

By Javier Martín · First published in Spanish on 1 July 2026 · English translation dated 27 November 2026 · Original edition

Listing on the stock exchange is an important milestone for many companies, but it is not without risks. In fact, there are important companies that refuse to go public to avoid certain problems of loss of control, excessive institutional oversight, …

That is why, in the robotics sector, we are surely going to see many kinds of situations. Small companies that go public very early, as in the case of Agility, which we present today, and companies that become very large without needing to go public.

Going public, for what matters to us, means being able to become shareholders in these companies we have been following and that can do so much to improve the world, so I think it is worth paying attention to.

In the case of Agility, it will go public through a SPAC, which usually has somewhat different connotations from a conventional listing, an IPO, as they call it in the USA, and OPV, as we call it here.

The SPAC thing is simpler than it seems. There are companies devoted to investment, in this case Churchill Capital, that list a kind of investment fund whose goal is to invest in a single company; the curious thing is that when this kind of fund goes public, the company to be invested in has not yet been chosen. Then part of the money raised is used to look for the company, and once it has been found, a stake in it is bought, and the one that remains listed is the "acquired" company, not the fund.

In this way small companies like Agility, which don't want to complicate their lives going through the whole IPO process, which is in turn quite expensive, can use this option and come to market quickly and easily to raise funds that allow them to accelerate their growth.

This way of listing became very popular in the USA from 2020 onwards and allowed many biotech and also quantum computing companies to go public. But precisely this previous history should put us on alert, because if we look at the share prices of those companies that listed through SPACs, we will see that in many cases retail shareholders have not benefited at all.

So history for now teaches us that we must be cautious, because the norm is that at such an early stage expectations are greatly inflated, and when the company begins to face public scrutiny many of those expectations collapse and the company's value can take many years to recover.

With Agility we have no idea what is going to happen in terms of market value, but what we do know is that the company is one of the pioneers in humanoid robotics and is taking the right steps to reach the market, for example thanks to its collaboration with Toyota, with Mercado Libre, with GXO, ...

And now let's move on to the rest of the market, because Agility is certainly not going to be an exception; in fact, an important piece of news has just occurred among Eastern robotics companies: SEER Robotics has begun trading on the Hong Kong stock exchange. This surprised me, since I expected Unitree or one of the other more advanced robot manufacturers to list first. For example, Agibot has also announced its plans to go public.

So where does all this interest in going public among robotics companies come from? It's simple: companies are following their usual life cycle, and in some cases even taking the fast track, as with Agility, but the truth is that the sector is at its best moment in terms of investor interest, and that must be taken advantage of.

The best moment is not my claim; it is that of the venture capital fund Adreessen Horowitz, which knows a thing or two about investing and has been warning for some time that after "software is eating the world" comes "robotics is going to be the largest industry in history".

In this way, robotics and physical AI already occupy second place in the startup investment ranking, surpassing other sectors with a longer track record such as Fintech, Biotech, Quantum, …

This is how the venture capital fund itself puts it:

"*The preference for "real world" technology is becoming evident in private markets, not only in AI infrastructure but also in robotics: if measured by the value of the top 100 private companies (by category), robotics (and physical AI) did not even appear on the list in 2016, but a decade later it has eclipsed fintech and payments as the second most important category. If you follow where venture capital investors' money is flowing, the rise in interest in robotics is also reflected in that flow. The first quarter of 2026 was a record for robotics and physical AI, both by value and by number of deals, with investment of approximately 16 billion dollars in just under 500 deals. To put it in context, the wave of robotics investment is roughly double in quantity and 4.5 times greater in value than the previous period between 2021 and 2025. The point is that the transition toward atoms (at least in private markets) is not limited to the chips and inference that will ultimately drive the next generation of software; hardware, as a product in itself, is booming. And it makes sense. Even better software has enormous potential, but robotics pushes technology toward a series of real-world "tasks" that software alone cannot tackle. To the extent that AI unlocks the software that drives hardware, it expands the reach of demand in unprecedented ways, similar to how electricity allowed machines to perform work humanity had barely imagined (before "power" was so readily available).*"

So now you know: we have more and more options to invest in Physical AI, which is good news for all of us who want to take advantage of the success of this sector.
